## Bridges — Donaukanal & Wien

### Schwedenbrücke (Schwedenplatz / Rotenturmstraße over the Donaukanal)
A 1368-built timber crossing in its first life ("Schlagbrücke"), iron-rebuilt 1818, steel-trussed 1864, dynamited in WWII, and rebuilt 1955 in the spare reinforced-concrete idiom of post-war recovery. Renamed "Sweden Bridge" in 1919 in gratitude for Swedish food-aid to starving Viennese children after WWI — a piece of humanitarian foreign policy welded into the city's street grid.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Aspernbrücke (Stubenring / Franz-Josefs-Kai over the Donaukanal)
The current 1864 cast-iron bridge replaced one of Vienna's first chain-suspension bridges (1864 original was actually the third structure here). Named for Archduke Karl's 1809 victory at Aspern-Essling — a bridge named for a battle won on the opposite bank of the Danube ten kilometres away. Walk it and you cross from the Innere Stadt into Leopoldstadt at the same point Roman ferries did.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Salztorbrücke (Morzinplatz / Salzgries over the Donaukanal)
A steel-girder beam bridge (rebuilt 1961) where the medieval Salt Gate of Vienna's city walls opened onto the river — salt barges from Hallein moored here for six centuries. The current bridge is unremarkable; the name is the artifact, encoding the economic engine (the salt trade) that paid for everything else in medieval Vienna.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Augartenbrücke — the lion pillars (Franz-Josefs-Kai / Obere Donaustraße)
The current 1928 concrete bridge replaced a chain-suspension bridge of 1872. The remarkable detail: four monumental stone pillars at the bridgeheads, each topped with a recumbent stone lion (Anton Hanak's school) — the lions face outward, guarding a crossing that is otherwise utilitarian. A modernist concrete deck on heraldic stone paws.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Friedensbrücke (Roßauer Lände / Klosterneuburger Straße)
"Peace Bridge" — opened 1926 to replace the WWI-era *Brigittabrücke* destroyed in flooding. The name was a deliberate political gesture by Red Vienna: a piece of public infrastructure renamed to embody the post-Habsburg republic's pacifist self-image. Rebuilt 1986 in steel; the U4 Friedensbrücke station shares the name.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Brigittabrücke / Rossauer Brücke (Roßauer Lände)
The original 1873 iron bridge here was named for Empress Maria Theresa's daughter, Archduchess Maria Christina. Destroyed in WWII; what stands today is a plain 1950s concrete crossing. The name "Brigittenau" for the entire 20th district came from a tiny 17th-century pilgrim chapel — the bridge encodes a saint, a district, and a vanished imperial bridge in one address.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Zollamtsbrücke / Zollamtssteg (Vordere Zollamtsstraße over the Wien)
Otto Wagner's 1898–1900 Stadtbahn bridge over the Wien river — a riveted steel arch carrying the U4 line, decorated with bronze laurel wreaths and the Habsburg double-eagle. The lattice ironwork is original Wagner-engineering, restored to its olive-green Stadtbahn livery. Walk underneath at the Stadtpark exit and the rivets are at eye-level.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Otto Wagner's Wientallinie viaducts (along the U4 corridor, Hütteldorf → Schönbrunn → Karlsplatz)
The U4 from Hütteldorf to Karlsplatz runs on Wagner's 1894–1899 Stadtbahn viaducts — a continuous 13-km Jugendstil railway built in five years by 8,000 workers. Olive-green ironwork, granite-block piers, glazed brick tunnels, Schimkowitz-style station heads. The line is the longest continuously preserved Jugendstil engineering work in Europe and you ride through it for €2.40.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Floridsdorfer Brücke (U6 over the Donau)
A 405-metre steel girder bridge (current structure 1978) carrying both road traffic and the U6 line — Vienna's only U-Bahn crossing of the main Danube where the train runs in the open on a road bridge. From the U6 window you get the full Danube panorama; from the Donauinsel below, you see the brutalist structural pylons that hold one of Europe's busiest tram-trains aloft.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Stadlauer Ostbahnbrücke (over the Donau, near Praterspitz)
A 1872 steel-truss railway bridge (rebuilt after WWII destruction, 1949) carrying the Ostbahn line east toward Bratislava. Approachable from the Donauinsel southern tip — you walk underneath the rust-coloured girders while freight trains rumble overhead. The bridgeheads still have masonry from the 1870 Vienna–Pressburg railway boom.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Donauinsel pedestrian bridges (Brigittenauer Brücke ramp, Steinspornbrücke, Ponte Cagrana)
A chain of cyclist/pedestrian bridges added piecemeal 1985–2001 to stitch the 21-km artificial island to both banks. The Steinspornbrücke (a wooden-decked beam bridge near the southern tip) is the loneliest; Ponte Cagrana floats over the New Danube on cable-stayed pylons. The whole island is an engineered hydraulic safety device disguised as a beach, accessed by bridges that exist because the island itself exists.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Locks & Water Control

### Kaiserwasser locks (Alte Donau, near Kaiserwasser bay)
The chain of weirs and small locks that 1870s engineers used to convert a meandering Danube arm into the still, swimmable Alte Donau lakes. Walk the Kaiserwasser embankment from U1 Alte Donau and you see the cylindrical sluice gates that froze a river in 1875. The Alte Donau is officially "standing water" by hydraulic decree — a piece of nature legislated into existence by these locks.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Donauinsel weirs — Einlaufbauwerk Langenzersdorf and Wehr 1 (Donauinsel northern tip)
Where the Neue Donau begins: a massive concrete inlet structure with seven steel sluice gates that open only during flooding to divert overflow from the main Danube into the relief channel. In normal weather the gates are sealed and the Neue Donau is a still lake. Walk to the island's north end and stand on top of a flood-control machine that protects Vienna from 100-year inundations.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wienflusseinwölbung (Stadtpark / Karlsplatz / Naschmarkt — multiple visible portals)
The Wien river was covered over 1895–1899 as part of Otto Wagner's Stadtbahn project — 4 km of river ducked underground, accidentally creating the Naschmarkt site (built on top of the lid). Visible at three portals: the Stadtpark Wienflussportal (downstream exit), under the Karlsplatz Stadtbahn pavilions (mid-section), and the upstream exit at Pilgramgasse. The Naschmarkt is technically a roof.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wienfluss-Auslaufportal (Urania / Donaukanal mouth)
Where the underground Wien empties into the Donaukanal, beside the Urania — a hooded stone-and-iron arch through which the river finally surfaces after its underground crossing of the city. Lean over the embankment railing and you watch a culverted river end its tunnel journey. After heavy rain the flow becomes terrifying.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## U-Bahn Engineering Hubs

### Karlsplatz U1/U2/U4 mezzanine
Three U-Bahn lines and the S-Bahn meet in a single 1970s subterranean mall that took two decades to dig — built around Wagner's preserved 1899 surface pavilions. The mezzanine ceiling is famously low (a 1970s cost-cutting decision) and the corridor running south to the Karlsplatz Resselpark exit is the longest pedestrian passage in any Vienna U-Bahn station: 280 metres of mosaic-tiled tube.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Stephansplatz U1/U3 — Virgilkapelle and exposed Roman walls
The U1/U3 transfer mezzanine includes a glass-walled archaeological viewing chamber: the 13th-century Virgilkapelle in its original 12-metre depth (rediscovered 1973 during U1 construction), plus exposed Roman-era cellar walls along the corridor between platforms. The Wien Museum operates the chapel as a sub-station of itself — a free archaeological exhibit you walk past on a Monday commute.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Volkstheater U2/U3 architecture
A 1980 station built deep enough to need three escalator banks — the lower platform is 26 metres below street level, the deepest U-Bahn point in central Vienna. The U2 line east of here was the city's first fully automated metro segment. The mezzanine is clad in 70s orange ceramic that is itself a listed period interior.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Westbahnhof U3/U6 hub and the 1952 station hall
Vienna's first major post-war public building (Robert Hartinger, 1949–52) — a clean modernist replacement for the bombed 1858 imperial terminus. The cantilevered concrete canopy over the entry, the freestanding clock tower, and the foyer's relief "Spirit of Travel" survive intact. Walk in for free, ride the escalators down to where the U6 platform sits on Wagner's old Vorortelinie viaduct.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Water Towers & Aqueduct Markers

### Wasserturm Favoriten (Windtenstraße 3, 1100)
An 1899 brick-and-sandstone water tower by Franz Borkowitz — 67 m tall, Romantic-historicist with corner turrets, copper roof, and a 1,000-cubic-metre tank near the top. Decommissioned 1910, kept as a listed monument because by then it had become an unmistakable Favoriten landmark. A piece of Victorian industrial decoration disguised as a fortress.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wasserturm Wienerberg (Triester Straße / Hertha-Firnberg-Straße, 1100)
A 1898 cylindrical brick tower that supplied the Wienerberg brickworks — the same factory that produced most of Vienna's Gründerzeit Ringstrasse buildings. The tower outlived the works (closed 1968); now a freestanding monument in a park-and-residential redevelopment, surrounded by 21st-century office towers it pre-dates by a century.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wasserbehälter Schmelz / Wasserturm Simmering (Kaiser-Ebersdorfer Straße, 1110)
The 1873 octagonal brick water tower for the southeast Vienna network — supplied by the *First Vienna Mountain Spring Pipeline* (Hochquellenwasserleitung), the 95-km gravity-fed aqueduct from the Schneeberg Alps to the city. The tower no longer holds water; it stands as a memorial to the 1873 engineering miracle that gave Vienna some of Europe's best tap-water.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Hochquellenwasser markers (scattered, e.g. Schwarzenbergplatz, Rax-Schneeberg route)
Cast-iron plates and small obelisks placed at intervals along the aqueducts and their city ends (the Hochstrahlbrunnen on Schwarzenbergplatz is the celebratory monument). The discreet plates carry the inscription "I. Wiener Hochquellenwasserleitung" and a date — a 95-km infrastructure project annotated like a museum exhibit. Look for them on Wienerberg, near reservoir Rosenhügel, and at Schwarzenbergplatz.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wasserbehälter Rosenhügel (Atzgersdorfer Straße, 1230)
The 1873 covered reservoir terminus of the First Mountain Spring Pipeline — a low brick structure topped by a public park (the Rosenhügel itself). You walk over 145,000 m³ of Alpine water without knowing it. The original neoclassical pump house is still there at the edge, in the strict architectural register of 1870s railway stations.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Fire Stations & Public Utility Buildings

### Zentralfeuerwache Am Hof (Am Hof 7–10, 1010)
Vienna's central fire brigade has occupied this corner of Am Hof since 1685 — making it possibly the world's oldest continuously-operating fire station on a single site. The 1908 façade still has the original watch-tower oriel from which observers spotted city fires before radios existed. The garage doors open onto the medieval-Baroque square; in any other city it would be a museum.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Feuerwache Mariahilf (Mariahilfer Gürtel 1, 1060)
A 1970s fire-station whose façade is aluminium-clad sheet metal — a "ship's hull" aesthetic deliberately echoing the modernist marine-engineering language of the period. The bay doors are oversized for 1970s ladder trucks; today's rigs barely clear them. A piece of post-war infrastructure that hasn't been demolished only because it works.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Heizwerk Arsenal (Arsenalstraße 6, 1030)
A 1960s district-heating plant whose decorated chimney visible from the Südbahnhof tracks is one of the few brutalist industrial structures left in central Vienna. Supplies hot water to ~40,000 households through underground pipes that snake from here under the Südbahnhof site to Favoriten. The chimney is 90 m tall and has navigation lights for aircraft.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Gas Lamps

### Surviving gas lanterns of the Innere Stadt (Heiligenkreuzerhof, Naglergasse, Schönlaterngasse area)
About 35 functioning gas streetlamps survive in Vienna — most clustered in the 1st district in atmospheric alleys deliberately preserved as a "historical lighting reserve" by the city. Each is hand-lit (with a photo-electric trigger) at dusk; the warm yellow glow is unmistakable next to LED street lighting. The Heiligenkreuzerhof courtyard still has a working gas lantern in its corner.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Gaslaterne plaque + the 1818/1843 gas network (Schottentor / Universitätsring area)
Vienna's gaslight network began in 1818 (the Hofburg arcades) and expanded to the city in 1843 — the Imperial-Royal Continental Gas Association ran 600 km of cast-iron pipes under the streets by 1900. A few enamel street signs still note "Gaslaternengasse"-type designations; look for the surviving wrought-iron gooseneck lampposts on Heldenplatz and around the Hofburg.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Public Drinking Fountains & Cast-Iron Lions

### Trinkbrunnen with cast-iron lion heads (across the Innere Stadt — e.g. Hoher Markt, Schwedenplatz, Stephansplatz periphery)
Vienna installed about 900 cast-iron public drinking fountains starting 1873 to celebrate the Hochquellenwasserleitung's debut — each in a standard design with a lion-head spout in bronze or iron, button-actuated. About 1,000 are still functional and tested daily by MA31. The water is Alpine, the lion is heraldic (Vienna's coat of arms is a single lion in a white shield), and the engineering is unchanged since Franz Joseph.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### "Schwemme" wall-mounted horse trough fountains (Heumarkt, Salzgries, Stallburggasse)
Smaller wall-mounted basins originally for cabhorses, repurposed as decorative drinking fountains after motorisation. The Heumarkt example (near the Konzerthaus) still has the iron tie-ring for reins. A piece of 19th-century traffic infrastructure that survived because nobody bothered removing it.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Clocks Beyond the Anker

### Generali-Turm / Generali-Tower clock (Landstraßer Hauptstraße 55, 1030)
The 1996 office tower's south face carries one of Vienna's largest contemporary public clocks — a clean steel-and-glass dial visible from the Wien Mitte transit hub. The clock is mechanically wound but synchronised by GPS, an engineering split between heritage and modern. Free to look at from the plaza below.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Kunsthistorisches Museum dome clock (Maria-Theresien-Platz)
The KHM's octagonal dome lantern carries four clock faces oriented to the cardinal directions — a small detail almost nobody notices because the dome's bronze statuary draws the eye upward. The mechanism is original 1891 Carl Hasenauer fitting. From the south fountain you can read the south face directly.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Hofburg Schweizertor sun-and-clock face (Schweizerhof, 1010)
Above the blood-red Renaissance Schweizertor sits a 16th-century painted clock face combined with astronomical-zodiac markings — a Habsburg device for displaying time, date, and saint's-day simultaneously. The clock mechanism is gone; the painted dial remains as evidence of an earlier idea of what a public clock should encode.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Pummerin announcement clock at Stephansdom north tower (Stephansplatz)
The famous Pummerin bell hangs in the north tower; less famously, the tower's exterior carries a small black-and-gold clock face that the Cathedral choirmaster uses to time the bell's hourly strike. The clock is offset 30 seconds from official Austrian time so the bell hits exactly on the hour. A clock calibrated to a bell, not a satellite.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Railway Engineering

### Stadtbahnbogen 1–48 / Gürtelbögen (along the U6 viaduct, Heiligenstadt → Westbahnhof)
Otto Wagner's 1898 Stadtbahn viaduct along the Gürtel was deliberately built with 48+ deep brick arches as ground-floor commercial space — a piece of 19th-century mixed-use engineering. Today the arches are a 4-km strip of bars, clubs, galleries, repair shops. Walk underneath at night and the viaduct functions exactly as Wagner specified, 130 years on.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Westbahnhof imperial-era interior columns and the 1950s replacement (Europaplatz 3, 1150)
The current station's foyer (1949–52) has a "Spirit of Travel" relief by Joseph Humplik on the entry wall and a freestanding clock-tower. Around the corner, a few surviving cast-iron columns of the 1858 imperial station are still in use in the underground tracks — visible from the U6 platform.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Südbahnhof traces — Quartier Belvedere (Arsenalstraße / Wiedner Gürtel, 1100)
The vast 1873 Südbahnhof terminus was demolished 2009–2010 to make room for the Hauptbahnhof, but its eastern trace remains: the curving Arsenalstraße rail wall, the 1955 rebuilt platforms now repurposed as park promenade, and the original imperial freight ramp at the southern edge. The Hauptbahnhof's space-frame canopy explicitly does NOT cover the old terminus footprint — the new station respects the ghost.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Franz-Josefs-Bahnhof (Julius-Tandler-Platz, 1090)
The least monumental of Vienna's terminus stations — a stripped 1970s rebuild over the 1872 original platforms. The interior columns and the curved track shed are still in use; from the platform end you see the line vanishing northwest toward Tulln and Český Velenice. Often forgotten because it serves only regional routes.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Power & Broadcast

### Umspannwerk Favoriten (Humboldtplatz, 1100)
A 1929 Wien Energie electrical substation by Eugen Kastner and Fritz Waage in stripped neoclassical brick — towers and high windows that look almost ecclesiastical. The building still steps down 110 kV to 20 kV for the south Vienna grid. A power-cathedral hiding in plain sight on a leafy square.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Umspannwerk Wien-Nord (Engerthstraße / Brigittenauer Lände, 1200)
A monumental 1929 substation by Eugen Kastner clad in fluted brick pilasters with bas-relief friezes representing electricity allegorically — a Genius with a thunderbolt over the entry. Listed as a heritage monument; still in service. One of the great unloved buildings of inter-war Vienna.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Donauturm broadcast role (Donauturmstraße 4, 1220)
Better known as a viewing tower (already in catalog), but the steel-lattice mast on top carries radio and TV transmitters serving the eastern half of Vienna — the tower's actual reason for existing in 1964. The official Austrian broadcasting authority ORF still rents space on the antenna ring; the revolving restaurant pays the mortgage.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Liesing-Inzersdorf high-voltage gate (Kledering, 1110/2334 border)
At the southeast edge of the city the 380 kV transmission lines from the Slovak grid first enter Vienna's distribution network — a fenced electrical compound the size of two football fields. From the Liesingbach cycle path you see the porcelain insulators stretching toward Bratislava. The hum is constant.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Sewers, Manhole Covers & The Third Man

### Karlsplatz sewer entrance — "Third Man Tour" trapdoor (Girardipark / Resselpark edge, 1010)
The actual descent point used in the 1949 *Third Man* sewer chase: a circular cast-iron manhole at the southwest edge of Girardipark, signposted today. The official Wien Kanal "Third Man Tour" descends here; from street level you can see the unique three-flange lid with the city seal. The cinematic mythology of Vienna ends — literally — at a manhole.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Vienna sewer manhole covers — lyre and city-seal grids (citywide, dense in 1010)
Vienna's standard cast-iron sewer covers carry a lyre symbol (the harp of the city's coat of arms, sometimes mistaken for a Habsburg device) plus the year of installation. Pavements in the Innere Stadt are an accidental atlas: 1898, 1923, 1957, 1989 — each cover dates the last time MA30 dug here. The oldest surviving covers near the Naglergasse are from the 1890s.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Sewer Museum / Third-Man-Museum exterior (Esterhazypark / Karlsplatz exit, 1060)
Wien Kanal operates a small visitor entrance on Girardipark — the surface portal of the "Third Man Tour" is a free-to-photograph sandstone arch over a stairway into the sewer system. The plaque tells the Greene-Reed story; the building itself is functional sewer infrastructure repurposed as cinema-history exhibit. After hours, just the iron grate remains.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wienfluss sewer overflow gates — the "monster mouths" (along the Wienfluss embankment, Stadtpark to Mariahilf)
The Wien river is also the city's combined-sewer overflow channel: during heavy rain, dozens of grated stone arches in the embankment walls vomit floodwater into the Wien. The arches are sized for biblical floods (post-1955 design standard) and have a permanent gothic-mouth aesthetic. From the U4 window between Schönbrunn and Karlsplatz you flash past them every few seconds.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## Telegraph & Post Office Artifacts

### Telegrafenamt building (Börseplatz 1 / Wipplingerstraße, 1010)
The 1873 imperial telegraph office by Carl Schumann — neo-Renaissance with a sandstone façade carrying carved laurel wreaths and the words "K.K. TELEGRAPHEN-AMT." Vienna's first cable to London ran from this address; the building is still partly owned by Austrian Post. The wrought-iron portico is unchanged since the morse-key era.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Otto Wagner mailbox — Postsparkasse exterior (Georg-Coch-Platz 2, 1010)
On the Postsparkasse's west façade, beside the entrance, sits a 1906 cast-aluminium mailbox designed by Wagner himself — same metal as the Postsparkasse's 17,000 aluminium bolts. Still in active use by Austrian Post: drop a letter and it falls through a Jugendstil portal. One of the only Wagner-designed objects you can put your hand on for free.
*Sources: infrastructure*
