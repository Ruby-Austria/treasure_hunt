# Vienna Locations — Master Catalog

Consolidated from nine research files (architecture, parks/squares, oddities, statues/monuments, cultural/literary, viewpoints, infrastructure, niche, outer_districts). All entries are outdoor, free to physically reach, GPS-friendly. Holocaust, Nazi-era, Jewish-trauma, composer-deathbed, gallows, plague-victim, and anonymous-drowning-victim memorials are excluded.

Format: `### Name (address)` — 2-3 sentence hook — *Sources*.

Districts run 1010 → 1230. Within a district, entries are clustered loosely by walking proximity.

---

## District 1010 — Innere Stadt

### Stephansplatz cluster

### Stock im Eisen (Stock-im-Eisen-Platz, corner Graben & Kärntner Straße)
A 1440-felled spruce trunk wrapped in iron bands and bristling with hundreds of nails, hammered in by passing locksmith apprentices for luck. Legend: the Devil himself locked it shut, and an apprentice sold his soul to forge an unbreakable padlock. The earliest documented nail dates to 1533 — a folk database of anonymous travelers encoded in iron, behind glass at a building corner that millions walk past without looking up.
*Sources: oddities, statues_monuments, parks_squares*

### Türkenkugel (south buttress of Stephansdom, Stephansplatz)
A 1683 Ottoman cannonball lodged in the cathedral wall — one of perhaps a thousand fired at the spire during the Second Turkish Siege, dated and left in place rather than removed. A small dark sphere that millions of pilgrims walk past without noticing.
*Sources: oddities*

### Riesentor & the Mammoth Bone (west front of Stephansdom)
In 1443, workers digging Stephansdom's foundation hit a colossal thighbone and hung it above the door, certain it belonged to a world-building giant — it was a mammoth. The bone now lives at the University; the gate kept the name "Riesentor" (Giant's Gate). Inscribed: 1443, and Frederick III's never-quite-decoded motto **AEIOU**.
*Sources: oddities*

### Heidentürme (Pagan Towers, west façade of Stephansdom)
Two stubby Romanesque towers that don't match the soaring Gothic spire. The "Heathen Towers" name comes either from being built of Roman rubble, from minaret-like silhouettes, or from the rumored pagan temple beneath — the oldest part of the cathedral, hiding in plain sight beneath the south tower's shadow.
*Sources: oddities*

### Wiener Elle (south wall of Stephansdom)
Two iron bars stick out of the cathedral wall at eye level — one for the linen ell, one for the woolen. Vienna's medieval municipal "you measured wrong" enforcement bureau, embedded in a cathedral wall so cloth traders could verify lengths in public.
*Sources: oddities*

### Pummerin clock face (Stephansdom north tower)
The famous Pummerin bell hangs in the north tower; less famously, the tower's exterior carries a small black-and-gold clock face used by the choirmaster to time the bell's hourly strike. The dial runs about 30 seconds offset from official Austrian time so the bell hits exactly on the hour — a clock calibrated to a bell, not a satellite.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Fiaker rank — Stephansplatz (1010)
Vienna's horse-drawn cabs park in two protected ranks where breed, livery, even the drivers' bowler hats are regulated by the 1815 *Fiakerordnung*. Each carriage carries a brass number; the horses wear protective hoof-pads on the cobbles. A 200-year-old guild operating as a living tourist attraction and an animal-welfare lightning rod simultaneously.
*Sources: niche*

### Equitable Palais passage (Stock-im-Eisen-Platz 3 → Goldschmiedgasse)
A grand Beaux-Arts passage cut through Andreas Streit's 1891 Equitable Insurance palace — coffered ceiling, marble walls, period brass shop-fronts. Connects Stock-im-Eisen-Platz to Goldschmiedgasse via what feels like a Parisian *galerie* somehow embedded one block from Stephansdom.
*Sources: niche*

### Bäckerstraße 7 Durchhaus (Bäckerstraße 7 → Sonnenfelsgasse)
A vaulted Renaissance courtyard with an arcaded loggia, painted in faded ochre — enter from the bakers' street and exit a block away on Sonnenfelsgasse. The loggia is one of the very few surviving Italian-style ones in central Vienna, a reminder that 16th-century Vienna was thick with Italian master-builders.
*Sources: niche*

### Trattnerhof passage (Graben 29 → Trattnerhof)
A barrel-vaulted shortcut from the Graben into a small inner court named for printer Johann Thomas Trattner, Mozart's landlord at one point. Barely two metres wide and almost invisible from the Graben crowd; pops out into a quiet courtyard with first-floor shops nobody finds by accident.
*Sources: niche*

### Stephansdom Sundial (south wall)
A faded medieval sundial painted on the cathedral wall, at human height — most people look up at the spire and miss it. Even when working, it was less accurate than the Anker Clock a few streets away: a 600-year-old broken clock on the most famous Vienna building.
*Sources: oddities*

### Virgilkapelle outline (Stephansplatz pavement, above the U-Bahn)
Outlined in lighter paving stones on the square is the floor-plan of a 13th-century underground chapel, rediscovered in 1973 during U-Bahn construction 12 metres below the cobbles. Walk over it and you're standing on a hidden medieval room.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Haas Haus (Stephansplatz 12)
Hans Hollein's 1990 postmodern mirrored drum, designed to reflect the cathedral back at itself. The bulbous Ottoman-flavored corner tower caused riots in the architecture press — a building that exists chiefly to mirror the building across from it.
*Sources: architecture*

### Mozarthaus / Figarohaus (Domgasse 5)
Mozart's only surviving Vienna apartment — the biggest, most expensive flat he ever lived in. He wrote *Le nozze di Figaro* here (1784–87) plus three "Haydn" string quartets. The plaque is unassuming; the alley still looks 18th-century, running behind Stephansdom in a hush.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Gutenberg-Denkmal (Lugeck)
Floppy-capped Gutenberg, one hand on hip, sculpted by Hans Bitterlich and unveiled 1900. The plinth bears the Latin motto **POST NUBILA PHOEBUS** — "after the clouds, the sun" — i.e. the printing press as enlightenment after medieval darkness.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Graben & Kohlmarkt cluster

### Graben (pedestrian street)
"Graben" literally means *trench* — this golden mile is the filled-in Roman defensive ditch outside Vindobona. Two facing fountains (Josefsbrunnen and Leopoldsbrunnen, 1804) bracket the street like exclamation marks. The whole baroque pedestrian zone is a buried fortification.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Ankerhaus (Graben 10)
Otto Wagner's 1895 prototype for the multi-use commercial block — shops with a two-storey glass curtain wall, offices, apartments, and a glass rooftop "photo atelier" perched like a greenhouse on top. Hundertwasser worked in that rooftop studio from 1971 until his death; one architect's prototype became another architect's nest.
*Sources: architecture*

### Kohlmarkt (Michaelerplatz end)
"Coal market" — a memory of when wood and charcoal vendors worked the gate of Roman Vindobona. Stand at the Michaelerplatz end and the entire street frames the green Michaelertor dome, a deliberate Baroque axis. Demel's confectionery at #14 served Franz Joseph and Sisi, and the Sachertorte rivalry still smolders.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Naglergasse — the Roman Wall Curve
A short street that bends sharply for no obvious reason — the bend traces the south-west corner of a Roman military camp (Vindobona) that vanished 1,800 years ago. The trench in front of it is now a street called Graben. Walking Naglergasse is walking along a Roman fortification you cannot see.
*Sources: oddities*

### Kleeblatthof / Naglergasse Durchhaus (Naglergasse 21 → Heidenschuss)
A medieval-feeling triple-courtyard sequence that links the Roman-curve street Naglergasse with Heidenschuss — three small cobbled courts in a chain, each smaller than the last. Locals use it as a wind-shelter shortcut in winter; tourists almost never find it.
*Sources: niche*

### Surviving gas lanterns (Heiligenkreuzerhof, Naglergasse, Schönlaterngasse alleys)
About 35 functioning gas streetlamps survive in Vienna — most clustered in the 1st district in atmospheric alleys deliberately preserved as a "historical lighting reserve" by the city. Each is hand-lit (with a photo-electric trigger) at dusk; the warm yellow glow is unmistakable next to LED street lighting. The Heiligenkreuzerhof courtyard still has a working gas lantern in its corner.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Café Hawelka (Dorotheergasse 6)
From outside, an unremarkable narrow shopfront — but in the 60s and 70s every interesting person in Vienna passed through this smoke-stained room. Artmann said without it "much would have remained undone, unsaid, or even unthought of." Leopold and Josefine Hawelka ran it themselves until they were nearly a century old.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Casanova Revuebühne (Dorotheergasse 6–8)
The cabaret club where Holly Martins traces Popescu in *The Third Man*, sharing a façade with Café Hawelka next door. Two worlds — coffee philosophers and cabaret racketeers — sharing one address.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Donnerbrunnen (Neuer Markt)
Georg Raphael Donner's 1739 fountain: Providentia at the center, four naked allegorical river-figures (Traun, Enns, Ybbs, March) around the basin. Maria Theresa was so scandalised by the nudity in 1773 she ordered them melted down — they survived and were returned in 1801. Possibly the first Viennese public art to ditch religious and Habsburg iconography.
*Sources: oddities, statues_monuments*

### Michaelerplatz & Hofburg cluster

### Michaelerplatz Roman Excavations
A sunken oval cut into the cobbles in front of the Hofburg, exposing Roman roads and medieval wall foundations 2,000 years deep — Hans Hollein's 1991 design. You walk around the rim and look down through layered centuries. Above it, the Hofburg dome; across, Loos's stripped-down façade.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities*

### Looshaus (Michaelerplatz 3)
Adolf Loos's 1910 façade so unornamented Emperor Franz Joseph allegedly called it "the house without eyebrows" and ordered the Hofburg curtains facing it kept closed. The city halted construction; Loos bolted on bronze flower boxes as a peace offering, and they're still there. The lower floors are clad in green Cipollino marble; the upper, in modernism's first manifesto.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Loos American Bar (Kärntner Durchgang 10)
A 27-square-metre bar tucked into a shopping passage off Kärntner Straße — Loos had lived three years in the US and built this in 1908 as a stained-glass American-flag panel above four blocks of marble. From outside it's almost a slot in the wall; inside, a mirror trick makes it feel infinite. Vienna's smallest world-famous building.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Schweizertor (Hofburg, Schweizerhof)
A blood-red and ink-black Renaissance gate from 1552 — the oldest portal of the Hofburg. The inscription lists every title Ferdinand I claimed that year in Roman numerals (MDLII). Despite the name, no Swiss soldier saw it until 200 years later under Maria Theresa. Above it sits a 16th-century painted clock face combined with astronomical-zodiac markings — a Habsburg device for displaying time, date, and saint's-day simultaneously; the mechanism is gone, but the painted dial remains.
*Sources: oddities, infrastructure*

### Hofburg stork chimney (In der Burg / Schweizerhof rooftop, 1010)
Real white storks return each spring to nest on a Hofburg chimney above the Schweizerhof — a centuries-old urban-rural anomaly visible from In der Burg courtyard, especially in April–May when chicks appear. Vienna's roof is, technically, a registered stork breeding site.
*Sources: niche*

### Lipizzaner stallion bronzes (Michaelerplatz / Reitschulgasse)
Outside the Stallburg and along Reitschulgasse, several life-sized bronze Lipizzaner heads and a full standing stallion mark the Spanish Riding School perimeter — the only place in the world where Renaissance High School dressage has run continuously since 1572. The horses themselves can sometimes be glimpsed crossing the cobbles between stable and arena at 09:00.
*Sources: niche*

### Pferdeschwemme — Stallburg horse-trough (Habsburgergasse)
A surviving Baroque stone horse-watering trough set into the Stallburg's ground-floor wall — once one of dozens scattered across Vienna for coach and fiaker horses, now one of the very few that remains in situ. The basin is dry but the carved imperial monogram is intact.
*Sources: niche*

### Stallburg (Reitschulgasse / Habsburgergasse)
A 1560s Renaissance palazzo wedged inside an otherwise Baroque city — and 72 Lipizzaner stallions still live on its ground floor. From the walkway you can sometimes see the horses' heads. The most expensively-housed mammals in Vienna live in a 460-year-old building.
*Sources: oddities*

### Spanish Riding School façade (Josefsplatz)
Fischer von Erlach's 1729 white riding hall — Europe's only place where classical dressage has been performed continuously since the 16th century. From Josefsplatz you stand where the school's predecessor (a wooden arena) used to be.
*Sources: oddities*

### Joseph II Equestrian Statue (Josefsplatz)
Franz Anton von Zauner spent twelve years (1795–1807) making this — Joseph II shown as a Roman conqueror in toga and laurel wreath, explicitly modelled on Marcus Aurelius on the Capitoline. Pure Habsburg cosplay: the dynasty claimed Roman-imperial descent and built the statue to prove it.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Heldenplatz / Volksgarten / Burggarten cluster

### Prince Eugene Equestrian Statue (Heldenplatz, south side)
Anton Fernkorn's 1865 bronze. Eugene of Savoy was refused a commission by Louis XIV (allegedly too short, too ugly), rode to Vienna in 1683, and became the empire's greatest general. The horse rears on its hind legs; trying to outdo himself after Archduke Karl across the square supposedly broke Fernkorn mentally — he spent the rest of his life in an asylum.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Archduke Karl Equestrian Statue (Heldenplatz, north side)
Fernkorn's 1860 engineering miracle: the horse balances on only two hind legs with no external support — first monumental bronze in the world to do this. Karl is depicted at the moment he seized a standard at Aspern-Essling, where he gave Napoleon his first land defeat. The pair makes Heldenplatz a sculptural dialogue: Habsburg vs Bourbon, Ottoman vs Napoleonic.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Heldenplatz central axis (Heldenplatz esplanade)
The broad gravel esplanade between the Neue Burg and the Volksgarten is the largest piece of empty horizontal civic space in Vienna — at its centre is an unmarked low circular paving feature where imperial parades historically aligned. Stand on it and the Burgtor arch frames the spire of the Rathaus exactly. Free, always open, surprisingly empty after dusk.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Burgtheater (Universitätsring 2)
Thomas Bernhard's *Heldenplatz* premiered here on 4 November 1988 to scheduled scandal — protesters dumped manure at the door, ministers walked out. The play indicts Austria's amnesia by setting itself on the square outside. The Ringstrasse-facing façade is the scene.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Volksgarten — Theseustempel
Pietro Nobile's 1820s exact recreation of the Temple of Hephaestus in Athens, built to house Canova's marble *Theseus Slaying the Centaur* — commissioned originally by Napoleon, then bought by Emperor Franz I. The statue moved to the Kunsthistorisches Museum in 1890, so the temple stands empty: a Greek shrine to a hero who is no longer there.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Volksgarten Rose Garden
Around 3,000 rose bushes in about 400 varieties — a botanical archive disguised as decoration. Late May is peak bloom. The Habsburgs literally turned a defensive earthwork (the demolished Burgbastei) into a perfumed garden.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Eichhörnchen-Statue (Volksgarten, near Theseustempel)
A small bronze squirrel set on a low stone — easy to miss, locals feed it acorns "for luck." One of Vienna's stealth animal monuments, more folklore-prop than commissioned sculpture.
*Sources: niche*

### Volksgarten Triton-Brunnen frogs and turtles
The Triton fountain and several Stadtpark basins carry bronze frogs, snakes, and turtles climbing the rim — added in the 1860s to make fountains "interesting at human eye-level for children." Vienna treated park fountains as zoology lessons; once you start looking you find amphibian fauna on a dozen basins.
*Sources: niche*

### Empress Elisabeth (Sisi) Memorial (Volksgarten east end)
Hans Bitterlich's seated Sisi in white Laas marble, unveiled 1907 — the last imperial monument erected in Vienna. Not as empress, but as a private woman: a book, roses, two dogs at her feet, only her first name on the inscription. A whole ritual "room" of reflecting pool and arcade most tourists walk past.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Grillparzer-Denkmal (Volksgarten, west side)
Carl Kundmann's 1889 Lasa-marble seated Grillparzer in a neoclassical exedra, flanked by six reliefs from his plays (*Sappho*, *Medea*, *König Ottokar*…). Bernhard wrote in *Alte Meister* about hating this monument from a Kunsthistorisches Museum bench across the Ring.
*Sources: statues_monuments, cultural_literary*

### Burggarten — Mozart-Denkmal
Viktor Tilgner's 1896 monument; Tilgner died five days before the unveiling, so his signature on the pedestal carries his death date. At Mozart's feet, the city gardeners plant a *living treble clef* in seasonal flowers — a musical score readable only in summer. The front relief is the famous Carmontelle scene of young Mozart at the harpsichord with his father and sister.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities, statues_monuments*

### Burggarten — Palmenhaus & Schmetterlinghaus
A 180-metre Jugendstil steel-and-glass cathedral by Friedrich Ohmann (1901–1907). The west wing now houses a tropical butterfly habitat, the east a brasserie — the same glass shell shelters Lepidoptera and Aperol Spritz. The building's curve mirrors the bastion that used to stand here.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Goethe-Denkmal (Opernring, facing Burggarten)
Edmund Hellmer's languid seated Goethe in his fifties, unveiled by Franz Joseph on 15 December 1900. He stares directly at the Schiller monument 180 metres away across six lanes of Ringstrasse traffic — Vienna's longest unblinking literary eye-contact.
*Sources: oddities, statues_monuments, cultural_literary*

### Schiller-Denkmal (Schillerplatz, in front of the Academy of Fine Arts)
Johannes Schilling's monument, unveiled on the poet's birthday — 10 November 1876. Schiller stands tall while Goethe sits; the four corner figures represent the four ages of life. Behind him: the academy that twice rejected a young applicant named Hitler, a fact the city doesn't advertise but you can verify from the steps.
*Sources: oddities, statues_monuments, cultural_literary*

### Maria-Theresien-Platz & Museumsquartier

### Maria Theresa Monument (Maria-Theresien-Platz)
Zumbusch's 19-metre 1888 bronze — fourteen years of construction. She holds the Pragmatic Sanction, the document that let her, a woman, inherit Habsburg lands. Four equestrian generals at mid-level; on the arts-and-sciences side, Gluck and Haydn stand behind her doctor, with Haydn's hand resting on the shoulder of a young boy who is Mozart. A whole court in bronze.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Maria-Theresien-Platz Triton & Mermaid Fountains
Four matching Carrara-marble fountains (1894) flank the empress's monument: Tritons, mermaids, children on rocks, shells and swans. Visitors look up at Maria Theresa's bronze throne and never count that there are four identical water mirrors arranged geometrically around her — a Habsburg compass rose.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### MuseumsQuartier (Museumsplatz 1)
One of the world's largest cultural districts shoehorned into Maria Theresa's former imperial stables, opened 2001. Two new boxes — the Leopold Museum (white shell-limestone cube) and MUMOK (a dark fortress in 13,000 basalt panels from Eifel volcanoes) — slot into the Baroque enfilade. The courtyards have the famous "Enzi" colored loungers nobody expected to become an icon.
*Sources: architecture*

### Am Hof / Freyung / Schottentor cluster

### Am Hof — Mariensäule
Built 1647, recast in bronze 1667. The Virgin spears a dragon at the top; at the base, four winged Putti slay a snake, a basilisk, a lion, and a second dragon — *heresy, plague, war, famine*. A whole apocalypse in cherub form, on a square sitting on Roman barracks and Babenberg castle foundations.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Am Hof Square
One of Vienna's largest cobbled squares, layered like an archaeological core: Roman garrison → Babenberg castle (1156) → market → Jesuit church where the end of the Holy Roman Empire was proclaimed in 1806. The pavement itself is the artifact.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Heidenschuss (off Freyung)
"Heathen Shot" — named for an old house sign showing a Saracen on horseback firing arrows. Folklore says during the 1529 Ottoman siege a baker's apprentice heard Turks tunneling beneath his cellar and raised the alarm. The current iron figure replaces the 1851 original; house-signs predate street numbers in Vienna and this is one of the few survivors.
*Sources: oddities*

### Freyung — Austriabrunnen
Ludwig Schwanthaler's 1846 fountain: Austria at the top, four reclining river-gods at her feet — Elbe, Vistula, Danube, Po, the watershed of the old empire compressed into one basin. Cast in Munich and shipped down the Danube; legend says smugglers tried to use the hollow figures to hide contraband cigars during transit.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Palais Daun-Kinsky (Freyung 4)
Built 1713–1719 by Hildebrandt — same architect as Belvedere — for Count Daun, sometime Viceroy of Naples and Governor of Milan. Compact, urban, with one of the most theatrical Baroque staircases in the city (visible from the open courtyard through the gateway). The palais whose name absorbed a countess.
*Sources: architecture*

### Palais Ferstel & Café Central (Strauchgasse 4 / Herrengasse 14)
Heinrich von Ferstel (1856–1860) kept his name on a Viennese palais — almost unique — because he'd just returned from Italy and grafted Venetian and Florentine motifs onto the Austrian National Bank. Now houses Café Central, where "Herr Bronstein" (Trotsky), Freud, Zweig, and Kokoschka argued for decades. The "Donaunixenbrunnen" (Danube mermaid fountain) sits in its glass-roofed passage.
*Sources: architecture, cultural_literary*

### Globe Museum / Esperanto Museum (Palais Mollard, Herrengasse 9)
One late-17th-century palace, two of the world's only specialised museums: 240 antique terrestrial and celestial globes (including Moon and Mars), and a museum of artificial languages (Esperanto, Volapük, Klingon). The façade gives no clue what's inside.
*Sources: oddities*

### Hoher Markt area

### Ankeruhr (Hoher Markt 10–11)
Franz Matsch's 1914 Jugendstil bridge-clock by Klimt's close associate, spanning two halves of the Anker Insurance HQ over Vienna's oldest square. Twelve historical figures parade across in 12 hours; at noon all twelve appear in sequence with period music — Marcus Aurelius leads, Haydn ends. A Habsburg cuckoo clock the size of a building.
*Sources: architecture, parks_squares, oddities*

### Roman Vindobona at Hoher Markt
Below this square sit the foundations of a Roman officer's house, uncovered during 1948–49 sewer work. The street layout itself encodes the camp: Graben, Naglergasse, Tiefer Graben, Salzgries, Rabensteig, Rotenturmstraße all trace its outer wall. The legion that camped here was the X Gemina.
*Sources: oddities*

### Hoher Markt — Vermählungsbrunnen
Vienna's oldest square (Roman forum site), with a 1732 fountain by Fischer von Erlach the Younger. The "Marriage Fountain" looks like it celebrates Habsburg dynastic union — but the figures are actually Mary and Joseph at their wedding before a high priest, an event recorded in no Bible and rarely depicted in art.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Schönlaterngasse cluster

### Schönlaterngasse (off Sonnenfelsgasse)
A curved Baroque alley named in 1780 after a single ornate iron lantern at No. 6 (the original now in the Wien Museum; a 1971 replica hangs in its place). The street bends because it traces a medieval property line — the whole alley feels like it belongs in a different city.
*Sources: oddities*

### Basiliskenhaus (Schönlaterngasse 7)
On 26 June 1212 a baker's apprentice in this courtyard found a basilisk — rooster-headed serpent with a fatal stare — at the bottom of the well, and killed it by holding up a mirror. Geologist Eduard Suess later proposed the cause was toxic gas, which is somehow better. The stone basilisk and a 1577 inscription (re-cut 1932) are at street level.
*Sources: oddities*

### Heiligenkreuzerhof (Schönlaterngasse 5 / Grashofgasse 3)
A silent Baroque courtyard owned since the 12th century by the Cistercian Abbey of Heiligenkreuz — an outpost of a forest monastery sitting in the middle of Vienna's inner city. Walk in through one alley, walk out a different one; the Bernardikapelle (1679) sits in the corner. Almost no tourists know it exists.
*Sources: oddities*

### Bermuda Triangle & Donaukanal edge

### Bermuda Triangle (Seitenstettengasse / Rabensteig / Sterngasse)
A medieval street tangle so disorientating that locals coined the nickname after the Atlantic Triangle: come in, get lost, vanish. The 1980s reinvented the area as Vienna's bar-crawl maze. By day, silent old town; by night, it eats itself. Streets look almost identical — which is the joke about GPS.
*Sources: oddities*

### Ruprechtskirche (Ruprechtsplatz)
A small Romanesque church traditionally dated to ~800 AD, patron saint of the Vienna salt merchants. The bells (c. 1280) are the oldest in the city; ivy creeps up the wall. It sits on the edge of the Bermuda Triangle nightlife zone, so the contrast at dusk is wild.
*Sources: oddities*

### Schwedenbrücke (Schwedenplatz / Rotenturmstraße over the Donaukanal)
A 1368-built timber crossing in its first life, iron-rebuilt 1818, steel-trussed 1864, dynamited in WWII, rebuilt 1955 in spare reinforced concrete. Renamed "Sweden Bridge" in 1919 in gratitude for Swedish food-aid to starving Viennese children after WWI — a piece of humanitarian foreign policy welded into the city's street grid.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Salztorbrücke (Morzinplatz / Salzgries over the Donaukanal)
A steel-girder beam bridge (rebuilt 1961) where the medieval Salt Gate of Vienna's city walls opened onto the river — salt barges from Hallein moored here for six centuries. The current bridge is unremarkable; the name is the artifact. A cantilevered pedestrian deck widens at midspan into a small terrace with built-in benches, low enough to see canal-level graffiti and high enough to read the curve toward the Uniqa Tower.
*Sources: infrastructure, viewpoints*

### Aspernbrücke (Stubenring / Franz-Josefs-Kai over the Donaukanal)
The current 1864 cast-iron bridge replaced one of Vienna's first chain-suspension bridges. Named for Archduke Karl's 1809 victory at Aspern-Essling — a bridge named for a battle won on the opposite bank of the Danube ten kilometres away. The eastern footpath has a widened semicircular bay with cast-iron railings, a 50-second pause looking upstream at the Uniqa Tower's LED skin reflecting off the water.
*Sources: infrastructure, viewpoints*

### Augartenbrücke lion pillars (Franz-Josefs-Kai / Obere Donaustraße)
The current 1928 concrete bridge replaced a chain-suspension bridge of 1872. Four monumental stone pillars at the bridgeheads, each topped with a recumbent stone lion (Anton Hanak's school), face outward — a modernist concrete deck on heraldic stone paws.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Zentralfeuerwache Am Hof (Am Hof 7–10)
Vienna's central fire brigade has occupied this corner of Am Hof since 1685 — possibly the world's oldest continuously-operating fire station on a single site. The 1908 façade still has the original watch-tower oriel from which observers spotted city fires before radios existed. The garage doors open onto the medieval-Baroque square; in any other city it would be a museum.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Telegrafenamt (Börseplatz 1 / Wipplingerstraße)
The 1873 imperial telegraph office by Carl Schumann — neo-Renaissance with a sandstone façade carrying carved laurel wreaths and the words "K.K. TELEGRAPHEN-AMT." Vienna's first cable to London ran from this address; the building is still partly owned by Austrian Post. The wrought-iron portico is unchanged since the morse-key era.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Otto Wagner mailbox (Postsparkasse west façade, Georg-Coch-Platz 2)
On the Postsparkasse's west façade, beside the entrance, sits a 1906 cast-aluminium mailbox designed by Wagner himself — same metal as the 17,000 aluminium bolts. Still in active use: drop a letter and it falls through a Jugendstil portal. One of the few Wagner-designed objects you can put your hand on for free.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Lion-head drinking fountains (Hoher Markt, Schwedenplatz, Stephansplatz periphery)
Vienna installed about 900 cast-iron public drinking fountains starting 1873 to celebrate the Hochquellenwasserleitung — each with a lion-head spout in bronze or iron, button-actuated. About 1,000 still functional today, tested daily by MA31. The water is Alpine, the lion is heraldic (Vienna's coat of arms is a single lion), and the engineering is unchanged since Franz Joseph.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wall-mounted horse-trough fountains (Heumarkt, Salzgries, Stallburggasse)
Smaller wall-mounted basins originally for cabhorses, repurposed as decorative drinking fountains after motorisation. The Heumarkt example (near the Konzerthaus) still has the iron tie-ring for reins — a piece of 19th-century traffic infrastructure that survived because nobody bothered removing it.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Vienna manhole cover atlas (citywide, dense in 1010)
Standard cast-iron sewer covers carry a lyre symbol (the harp of the city's coat of arms) plus the year of installation — Habsburg double-eagle (pre-1918), First Republic, Red-Vienna chevrons, post-1955 Wiener Stadtwerke star. Pavements in the Innere Stadt are an accidental atlas: 1898, 1923, 1957, 1989. The oldest surviving covers near Naglergasse are from the 1890s.
*Sources: infrastructure, niche*

### Stephansplatz U-Bahn — Virgilkapelle viewing chamber
The U1/U3 transfer mezzanine includes a glass-walled archaeological viewing chamber: the 13th-century Virgilkapelle in its original 12-metre depth (rediscovered 1973 during U1 construction), plus exposed Roman-era cellar walls along the corridor between platforms. The Wien Museum operates the chapel as a sub-station of itself — a free archaeological exhibit you walk past on a Monday commute.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Franz West — Vier Lemurenköpfe (Stubenbrücke parapet)
Four pink-orange aluminium heads by Vienna's most internationally famous late-20th-century sculptor, mounted on the Stubenbrücke parapet over the Wien-Donaukanal junction (1999). Cartoon-grotesque, deliberately ugly, beloved. West also placed *Privatlampe des Künstlers III* and other works around the city — a treasure hunt within a treasure hunt.
*Sources: niche*

### Maria am Gestade (Salvatorgasse 12)
A delicate Gothic church on a hill that used to be a Danube riverbank — until the river was diverted and the bank became dry land. The nave is built skewed because the ground was too tight; the 56-metre openwork tower (1419–1428) is unique in Vienna. Holly Martins flees down the staircase in *The Third Man*; Jesse and Céline walk past in *Before Sunrise*.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Altes Rathaus — Andromedabrunnen courtyard (Wipplingerstraße 8)
The last work of Georg Raphael Donner (1741), in the courtyard of the Old City Hall — lead relief, not bronze, with Donner's signature soft modelling. Andromeda chained to her rock; Perseus rescuing her. The Habsburgs once forced Donner to recast a nude *Providentia* (now in the Belvedere) because the church found it indecent — this Andromeda is similarly bold.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Mölker Bastei / Schreyvogelgasse cluster

### Pasqualatihaus & Mölker Bastei (Mölker Bastei 8)
A block of houses perched on a surviving fragment of Vienna's 16th-century city wall — the bastion is still there under your feet. Beethoven rented apartments here on and off 1804–1814, composing the 4th, 5th, and 7th Symphonies, *Fidelio*, the Archduke Trio, and *Für Elise*. The walls are literal fortification stone.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities, cultural_literary*

### Schreyvogelgasse 8 — the Third Man doorway
The doorway where Orson Welles steps out of shadow, a cat at his shoelaces (pilchard oil bait), in arguably cinema's most famous reveal. Linklater later used it in *Before Sunrise*. A film-history Easter egg hiding on a tiny side street — and 30 seconds from where Beethoven wrote the Fifth Symphony.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Drei-Mäderl-Haus (Schreyvogelgasse 10)
A Biedermeier house immortalised in a 1916 operetta about Schubert and three fictional sisters — pop-culture mythology so successful it shaped how Vienna remembered Schubert. The plaque is sentimental fluff, next door to the *Third Man* doorway.
*Sources: oddities*

### Augustinerbastei / Albertina

### Erzherzog Albrecht Equestrian (Augustinerbastei, beside Albertina)
Zumbusch's 1899 bronze (7,000 kg) on the highest pedestal-bastion in central Vienna — one of the only surviving bastions of Vienna's demolished city walls. From this terrace you can see the State Opera, the Albertinaplatz, and a cross-section of the layer-cake of imperial Vienna.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Augustinerkirche & the Herzgruft hint (Augustinerstraße 3, Josefsplatz)
Stand outside this church and you're standing above 54 silver urns containing Habsburg hearts. Body to the Capuchins, heart to the Augustinians, intestines to Stephansdom's catacombs — the Habsburgs literally split themselves across three Vienna buildings.
*Sources: oddities*

### Kapuzinergruft entrance door (Tegetthoffstraße 2)
The façade door where the Habsburg knock-ritual happens: master of ceremonies knocks three times, the Capuchin asks *"Wer begehrt Einlass?"* The titles are recited, refused; only when the candidate is named as "a poor sinner" is the door opened. The church façade is austere by Capuchin vow — deliberately humble next door to imperial Vienna. Carol Reed shot a *Third Man* scene here.
*Sources: oddities*

### Café Mozart (Albertinaplatz 2)
Graham Greene drafted scenes of *The Third Man* sitting here in 1948, and Anton Karas's zither theme is named "The Café Mozart Waltz." Joseph Cotten and Alida Valli's characters meet here in the film. There's no plaque marking this — only the name on the awning.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Café Sacher & Hotel Sacher (Philharmonikerstraße 4)
The Sacher was requisitioned as British officers' HQ in 1945–49, and it's where Holly Martins is summoned by Major Calloway in *The Third Man*. Eduard Sacher's wife Anna ran the hotel for decades amid cigar smoke and French bulldogs, courting archdukes who slipped in for actresses. The torte recipe is still litigated.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Burgkino (Opernring 19)
A standard mid-century Vienna cinema by appearance, but it has shown *The Third Man* in English nearly every week for over seventy years. Tuesday, Friday, Sunday. Cash only. The film never closes.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Secession Building ("Golden Cabbage", Friedrichstraße 12)
Olbrich's 1898 art temple, topped by a dome of 2,500 gilded iron laurel leaves — locals immediately nicknamed it *Goldenes Krauthappel*, the "Golden Cabbage." Above the door: *"Der Zeit ihre Kunst, der Kunst ihre Freiheit."* The laurel is for victory, the owls flanking the door for Athena: a temple of art that pretends to be a mausoleum.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Otto Wagner Postsparkasse (Georg-Coch-Platz 2)
The first modernist office building in continental Europe (1904–1906). Wagner clad the façade in thin marble panels held on by ~17,000 aluminium bolts — the bolts are not structural, they are the ornament. Aluminium was a luxury metal in 1906, more expensive than silver a generation before. Two aluminium angels by Schimkowitz stand on the parapet.
*Sources: architecture*

### Zacherlhaus (Brandstätte 6 / Wildpretmarkt)
Jože Plečnik's only major Vienna work (1903–1905) before he left for Prague and Ljubljana. Vertical polished grey granite slabs hung as non-load-bearing cladding — a technique that predates Wagner's own Postsparkasse façade. An Archangel Michael on the corner; muscular atlantes by Franz Metzner crouch under the eaves. The building where Wagner's student out-Wagnered Wagner first.
*Sources: architecture*

### Café Korb Philosophicum entry (Brandstätte 9)
Beside Café Korb, an unmarked door leads down to the *Philosophicum* — a basement room where philosophers, novelists, and Susanne Widl's invited eccentrics hold public talks. The entry detail is the artwork: it looks like a service door.
*Sources: niche*

### Würstelstand am Hohen Markt (Hoher Markt)
The other contender for Vienna's classic Würstelstand — operating since 1928 on the corner of Vienna's oldest square, directly below the Anker Clock. The kiosk's enamel signage and white-tiled counter survived four eras of regime change.
*Sources: niche*

### Bitzinger Würstelstand Albertinaplatz (Augustinerstraße 1)
The most architecturally photographed sausage kiosk in Vienna — a green-and-cream metal box wedged between the Albertina ramp and the State Opera. Open until 04:00, serving Käsekrainer with Champagne. Declared "intangible cultural heritage of Austria" by UNESCO Austria — yes, the sausage kiosk is heritage.
*Sources: niche*

### Jugendstil pissoir (Graben)
A green wrought-iron Art Nouveau public urinal kiosk on the Graben — Wilhelm Beetz, c. 1905, one of only a handful surviving of dozens once scattered across Vienna. Functional, ornate, recently restored. Vienna treated even its public toilets as Secessionist design objects.
*Sources: niche*

### Café Korb (Brandstätte 9)
Opened 1904 with Franz Joseph at the ribbon. Owner Susanne Widl is herself a 1970s conceptual-art figure — she runs a café where Jelinek (Nobel 2004) drinks at a designated table while a "Philosophicum" of public philosophy runs downstairs. A Vienna café that is also an ongoing artwork.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Engel-Apotheke (Bognergasse 9)
Oskar Laske's 1901 pharmacy whose sign is a Jugendstil mosaic of two angels carrying glowing vessels — one of the only surviving Art Nouveau pharmacy façades in Vienna. The "Zum weißen Engel" name dates to 1587; the current building rebuilt the apothecary's medieval identity in gold-leaf and ceramic at the precise moment Vienna invented modern advertising.
*Sources: architecture*

### Franziskanerplatz — Mosesbrunnen
A tiny L-shaped square hidden behind the Franciscan church, almost invisible from any main thoroughfare. The 1798 Moses Fountain by Johann Martin Fischer shows Moses striking the rock; next door is Kleines Café, said to be the smallest coffeehouse in Vienna. A pocket square that feels like a film set.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Rooftop Remodeling Falkestraße (Falkestraße 6)
Officially "the world's first realized deconstructivist building" (Coop Himmelb(l)au, 1983–1988) — a metal-and-glass creature that looks like it crashed through the roof of a 19th-century lawyer's office. The mayor said it looked "more like art than architecture"; the architects said thank you, because art isn't bound by building code. Shown at MoMA's 1988 Deconstructivism exhibition.
*Sources: architecture*

### Ring & Parliament

### Pallas-Athene-Brunnen (Dr.-Karl-Renner-Ring 3)
A 5.5-metre Athena in gilded helmet holding Nike on her palm in front of the Greek-revival Parliament — Theophil Hansen's design, Carl Kundmann's sculpture, completed 1902. Four owls (wisdom) on her column's capital; four reclining river-gods (Danube, Inn, Elbe, Vltava) at the basin. Local joke: Athena turns her back on Parliament because she's disgusted by what happens inside.
*Sources: architecture, parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Rathausmann (Rathaus, Friedrich-Schmidt-Platz)
A 5.4-metre iron standard-bearer 98 metres above the city, cast from melted-down Russian war-reparation kopeks. His armour copies Maximilian I's. He weighs 650 kg, balanced by an 800 kg pendulum-counterweight hidden in the spire so he sways in storms. Shoe size: 63 (UK 29). A 1:1 replica stands in the Rathauspark at ground level.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Rathauspark
Two symmetric halves north and south of the Rathaus, each with its own fountain, vast old trees, and meandering paths. The "park inside the Ring" most overlooked because the Rathaus building itself overshadows it. In May, lilacs and chestnut blossoms anchor the experience.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Sigmund Freud Park & Memorial (Universitätsring, opposite Votivkirche)
The park is the monument — no figural bust, only a bench and a stone tablet inscribed with Greek Ψ Α (Freud's shorthand for psychoanalysis) and his quote *"Die Stimme des Intellekts ist leise"* — "The voice of the intellect is a soft one." Freud passed it daily walking from Berggasse 19; the city named the grass after him in 1985 as deliberate anti-monument.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments, cultural_literary*

### University of Vienna Arkadenhof (Universitätsring 1)
An arcaded Renaissance-revival courtyard ringed by 150+ busts of professors — Doppler (1901), Boltzmann (his entropy formula on the bust), Schrödinger, Freud (added belatedly in 1955 after long resistance). A free open-air pantheon of Austrian intellectual history, all glaring at one another across a fountain.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Café Landtmann (Universitätsring 4)
Freud's café of choice — he'd cross Schottentor from Berggasse 19 to read papers here. It's also where Austrian politics still happens: ministers brief journalists at these tables. The terrace faces the Burgtheater across the Ring.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Café Prückel (Stubenring 24)
Opened Christmas Day 1903 by a former champion bicycle racer. Oswald Haerdtl's 1955 redesign of the front room is itself a protected work of mid-century modern. Sunday afternoon piano salon still runs.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Café Schwarzenberg (Kärntner Ring 17)
The first café built into the Ring after the city walls came down (1861) — 165 years of Vienna walking past its windows. Coachmen of the imperial guard once tied horses outside; today the same façade looks across to a fountain. The least disguised time-capsule on the Ring.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Radetzky-Denkmal (Stubenring, in front of the Ministry of Defence)
Zumbusch's 6,000 kg bronze, originally on Am Hof, moved to Stubenring in 1912. At the original unveiling, Strauss Sr.'s *Radetzky March* was played — the only military monument with a built-in soundtrack. Reliefs show Radetzky receiving acclaim from soldiers of every branch of the multi-ethnic army.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Ringturm (Schottenring 30)
Postwar Vienna's first high-rise (1955) — 73 m, with a 20 m "weather beacon" on top whose 117 coloured lamps signal tomorrow's forecast by blinking patterns almost no one in Vienna can still read. Since 2006 the tower has been annually wrapped in a 4,000 m² artwork by a different artist from a country where Vienna Insurance Group operates. A building whose skin changes once a year and whose hat predicts the weather.
*Sources: architecture*

### Karlsplatz cluster

### Karlskirche (Karlsplatz 10)
A Baroque church (Fischer von Erlach, begun 1716) flanked by two 47-metre bas-relief columns directly modeled on Trajan's Column — spiralled with the life of St. Charles Borromeo. The portico is a Greek temple, the dome is Roman, the columns are Imperial, the flanking pavilions look Islamic. A single building making the explicit claim that Habsburg Vienna is the legitimate fourth Rome.
*Sources: architecture, oddities, statues_monuments*

### Karlsplatz Stadtbahn Pavilions
Two tiny apple-green-and-white Otto Wagner pavilions (1898–99) facing each other across the square, decorated with gold sunflowers and Schimkowitz's "Crying Women" heads on the roofline. The surface-level remnants of Vienna's old Stadtbahn — sat at angles to nothing in particular because the tracks beneath moved. One is a café, the other an exhibition space.
*Sources: architecture, parks_squares, oddities, statues_monuments*

### Resselpark & Henry Moore "Hill Arches"
A pond in front of Karlskirche holds Henry Moore's *Hill Arches* (1973), donated to the city in 1978. The reflecting pool doubles the Karlskirche dome — a visual trick set up by post-war urban planners. Brahms (seated, brooding) and Siegfried Marcus (automotive pioneer) anchor the eastern park. A gently raised gravel path on the south edge — three steps up — lines up the two Wagner pavilions, the Moore pool, and the Karlskirche dome in a single sightline; you're walking on the lid of the culverted Wienfluss.
*Sources: parks_squares, viewpoints*

### Karlsplatz U-Bahn mezzanine (Karlsplatz)
Three U-Bahn lines and the S-Bahn meet in a single 1970s subterranean mall, built around Wagner's preserved 1899 surface pavilions. The mezzanine ceiling is famously low (1970s cost-cutting) and the corridor running south to the Resselpark exit is the longest pedestrian passage in any Vienna U-Bahn station: 280 metres of mosaic-tiled tube.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Third Man sewer trapdoor (Girardipark / Resselpark edge)
The actual descent point used in the 1949 *Third Man* sewer chase: a circular cast-iron manhole at the southwest edge of Girardipark, signposted today. The official Wien Kanal "Third Man Tour" descends here; from street level you can see the unique three-flange lid with the city seal. The cinematic mythology of Vienna ends — literally — at a manhole.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Brahms-Denkmal (Resselpark / Karlsplatz)
Rudolf Weyr beat Max Klinger in an international competition for this 1908 commission. Brahms sits enthroned in marble; below him the muse Euterpe plucks a lyre in despair. The statue sits between Brahms's last apartment (Karlsgasse 4, no longer standing) and the Musikverein where he worked his last decade — he walked past this spot daily.
*Sources: statues_monuments, cultural_literary*

### Beethoven-Denkmal (Beethovenplatz)
Zumbusch's 1880 bronze. Beethoven sits like Michelangelo's Moses on a high plinth, flanked by Prometheus (the suffering Titan) and Nike (eternal glory). Putti below personify the symphonies. 19th-century critics called the Prometheus "the embodiment of restless human longing."
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Hochstrahlbrunnen (Schwarzenbergplatz)
A fountain that is a calendar in water (1873): 365 small jets for days, 12 high jets for months, 24 medium jets for hours, 6 mid-size for weeks, 30 for days-of-month. Built in four months to celebrate the First Vienna Mountain Spring Pipeline that finally brought Alpine water to the city. Runs late March to late October — when the central 30 m jet is active (daily 11:00–22:30), it is visible from the Belvedere upper terrace 600 m southeast and the Stadtpark Wienfluss bridge 400 m northeast. Discrete cast-iron "I. Wiener Hochquellenwasserleitung" markers along the Ringstrasse and at Wienerberg / Rosenhügel annotate the 95-km aqueduct that this fountain celebrates.
*Sources: architecture, parks_squares, statues_monuments, viewpoints, infrastructure*

### Schwarzenberg Equestrian Monument (Schwarzenbergplatz north)
Ernst Hähnel's 1867 bronze, commissioned by Franz Joseph to honour the man who co-led the coalition that beat Napoleon at Leipzig in 1813 and marched into Paris — the closing chapter of the Napoleonic Wars rendered in stone.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Stadtpark cluster

### Stadtpark — Johann Strauss "gilded violinist"
Edmund Hellmer's 1921 bronze, gold-plated on the outside — Vienna's most-photographed monument. The gold leaf was stripped off in 1935 (Depression austerity) and re-gilded only in 1991: for 56 years the "Golden Strauss" was just dull bronze. Behind him, a marble arch with sculpted naiads frozen in mid-waltz.
*Sources: parks_squares, statues_monuments*

### Stadtpark — Schubert-Denkmal
Carl Kundmann's 1872 Carrara-marble Schubert, overseen by his friend the painter Moritz von Schwind — Schubert as someone who knew him remembered him. Reliefs personify "Imagination" (front), "Instrumental Music" (left), "Vocal Music" (right). Funded by the Vienna Men's Choral Society over a decade of charity concerts.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Stadtpark — Anton-Bruckner-Büste
Viktor Tilgner started this 1891 bust but died before finishing — a third party completed it, the same pattern as Tilgner's Mozart-Denkmal. The original muse-of-music base was smashed by vandals; the current pedestal is a 1988 replacement.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Stadtpark — Franz-Lehár-Denkmal
The odd one out in the Stadtpark composer collection: every other monument is 19th-century historicist, but Lehár (Franz Coufal, 1980) gets a Cubist marble triangle stack behind a stern bust. *Merry Widow* energy in stone.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Stadtpark — Donauweibchen-Brunnen
Hanns Gasser's 1862 Carrara-marble Danube Maiden — a friendly river-nymph from Viennese folklore who warned fishermen of floods and brought treasure from the riverbed. The folds of her topless drapery hold two fish, referencing the city's old Fischhof market. The 1948 stone copy (after WWII damage) is what stands today.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities, statues_monuments*

### Stadtpark — Befreiung der Quelle ("Liberation of the Spring")
Josef Heu's 1903 Symbolist fountain in Joseph Urban's ensemble: two nude males, only half-emerging from the rock, strain to lift the boulder blocking the spring. Reads like Michelangelo's *Prisoners* fused with Art Nouveau. Urban later moved to America and designed for Ziegfeld and the Met.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Wienflussportal (Stadtpark)
Friedrich Ohmann and Josef Hackhofer's 1903–1906 Jugendstil masterpiece: a curved hemicycle of staircases, balustrades, and twin domed pavilions where the underground Wienfluss surfaces. The original plan called for water-spouting bronze elephants and a veil-waterfall over the keystone — both cut for budget. Below the arch, the U4 thunders past on Otto Wagner's Stadtbahn line. The Wienfluss was covered over 1895–1899 as part of Wagner's Stadtbahn project — 4 km of river ducked underground, with portals also visible at Karlsplatz and Pilgramgasse; the Naschmarkt is technically the river's roof.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities, infrastructure*

### Stadtpark — Karpfenbrunnen
A small carp fountain tucked near the Wienflussportal — bronze fish spitting water into a low pool. Easy to miss, but a Vienna folk-touchstone: throw in a coin and your wish (according to local children) gets carried into the underground Wienfluss to the Danube.
*Sources: niche*

### Wienfluss sewer overflow "monster mouths" (Wienfluss embankment, Stadtpark to Mariahilf)
The Wien river is also the city's combined-sewer overflow channel: during heavy rain, dozens of grated stone arches in the embankment walls vomit floodwater into the Wien. The arches are sized for biblical floods (post-1955 design standard) and have a permanent gothic-mouth aesthetic — from the U4 window between Schönbrunn and Karlsplatz you flash past them every few seconds.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Zollamtsbrücke / Zollamtssteg (Vordere Zollamtsstraße over the Wien)
Otto Wagner's 1898–1900 Stadtbahn bridge over the Wien river — a riveted steel arch carrying the U4 line, decorated with bronze laurel wreaths and the Habsburg double-eagle. The lattice ironwork is original Wagner-engineering, restored to its olive-green Stadtbahn livery; walk underneath at the Stadtpark exit and the rivets are at eye-level.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wienfluss-Auslaufportal (Urania / Donaukanal mouth)
Where the underground Wien empties into the Donaukanal beside the Urania — a hooded stone-and-iron arch through which the river finally surfaces after its underground crossing of the city. Lean over the embankment railing and watch a culverted river end its tunnel journey. After heavy rain the flow becomes terrifying.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## District 1020 — Leopoldstadt

### Tegetthoff-Denkmal (Praterstern)
A naval-victory monument in landlocked Vienna: an 11-metre marble column crowned by a 3.5-metre bronze admiral, the shaft studded with six bronze ram-bows of warships — a Roman *columna rostrata* commemorating the 1866 Battle of Lissa over the Italian navy. A 600-km inland tribute to a sea victory the empire can no longer reach.
*Sources: oddities, statues_monuments*

### Nestroy-Denkmal (Nestroyplatz / Praterstraße 17)
Oskar Thiede's 1929 statue of Vienna's satirical playwright as the character Blasius Rohr — a monument that itself has a picaresque biography: hidden in the Max-Reinhardt-Seminar courtyard for thirty years, exiled to Penzing, finally returned 1983. Nestroy was the Mark Twain of Biedermeier Vienna.
*Sources: statues_monuments, cultural_literary*

### Wiener Riesenrad (Riesenradplatz, Prater)
Built 1897 by a British Royal Navy engineer. Only every second gondola was rebuilt after a 1944 fire — hence the strangely large gaps between cars today. Site of cinema's most famous monologue: Welles improvising "in Italy for 30 years under the Borgias… they produced Michelangelo. In Switzerland they had brotherly love, 500 years of democracy and peace, and what did they produce? The cuckoo clock." The wheel is best viewed *from below* as a landmark from a low grassy hummock 200 m west in the open Volksprater fairground — from there the sparse comb of gondolas reads against the sky, the empty gaps the historical detail.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary, viewpoints*

### Liliputbahn miniature railway (Prater Hauptallee)
A 4-km miniature steam-and-diesel railway running since 1928 around the Prater on 381 mm gauge — three working stations with platforms, signals, and a proper turning loop. Trains run April–October. The locomotives are real coal-fired engines, just 1.2 metres tall.
*Sources: niche*

### Ernst-Happel-Stadion exterior (Meiereistraße 7)
Austria's national stadium, opened 1931 as *Praterstadion* — capacity 50,000, host of the 2008 Euros final. The unusual oval colonnade silhouette is visible from the Prater Hauptallee; on non-match days you can walk the entire perimeter and read the bronze panels listing every Austria–Hungary national-team result.
*Sources: niche*

### Wiener Kriminalmuseum exterior (Große Sperlgasse 24)
Vienna's true-crime museum in the 17th-century *Seifensiederhaus* (soap-boilers' house) — itself the oldest civic building in Leopoldstadt. The exterior alone is worth the visit: a half-timbered Baroque survivor on a street of postwar blocks.
*Sources: niche*

### Donaukanal graffiti mile (Schwedenplatz → Friedensbrücke, 1020/1090)
One of Europe's longest officially-legal graffiti zones — the embankment wall is repainted weekly and operates as a rotating outdoor gallery. Crews from Skero to Nychos to international writers leave layered work; the stretch beneath Augartenbrücke is the densest.
*Sources: niche*

### Republic of Kugelmugel (Antifaschismusplatz 2, Prater)
A spherical wooden house built by Edwin Lipburger in 1971, declared its own micronation when Austrian authorities ruled spherical buildings illegal. Lipburger issued passports, was jailed for fake stamps, was pardoned, moved to the Prater in 1982, fenced with barbed wire. Reopened 2024 after restoration. A round, sovereign nation behind a fence in an amusement park.
*Sources: oddities*

### Augarten (Obere Augartenstraße)
Vienna's oldest baroque garden, opened to the public by Joseph II in 1775 — free ever since. The park is shaped like a kite; inside sits the 1705 porcelain manufactory (Europe's second-oldest, founded 1718). The 18th-century park wall encircles it for almost a kilometre, perfectly preserved. The corner garden gate at Obere Augartenstraße 1 opens into a small forecourt with a fountain shaped like a Habsburg porcelain teapot scaled to architectural size — a self-quoting building.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities, outer_districts*

### Prater Hauptallee
A 4.5-km arrow-straight allée lined with ~2,500 chestnut trees. Joseph II opened the Prater to the public in 1766 — an Enlightenment gesture so radical that nobles protested for losing their private hunting grounds. In late May the chestnuts are in full bloom, white candles glowing over the path — for three weeks the crowns close into a continuous green tunnel-canopy 15 m above your head, and the avenue becomes a horizontal cathedral.
*Sources: parks_squares, viewpoints*

### Prater — Lusthaus (Freudenau 254)
An octagonal Habsburg pleasure pavilion at the far end of the Hauptallee — originally an imperial hunting outpost rebuilt 1781–1783 by Isidore Canevale. It marks the geographical vanishing point of the avenue: walk 4.5 km straight, and you arrive at this small yellow octagon in a clearing.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Karmelitermarkt + Karmeliterplatz (Im Werd)
Smaller and quieter than Naschmarkt; revived in the 2010s as a creative-class neighborhood market. Fewer stalls, more atmosphere, with cafés spilling out next to the 17th-century Carmelite church. Saturday mornings are the moment.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Uniqa Tower (Untere Donaustraße 21)
A 75 m office tower (2004) whose floor plan is the letter Q. The entire curved façade is a 7,000 m² LED media wall with ~160,000 individual LEDs — Vienna's biggest screen, programmed nightly with abstract light-art that morphs slowly along the Donaukanal.
*Sources: architecture*

### WU Library and Learning Center (Welthandelsplatz 1)
Zaha Hadid's 42,000 m² polygonal block (2013) whose roof cantilevers 25 m over the plaza — a stone wedge pushed off the cliff it sits on. The "monitor face" glass façade points at the Prater amusement park as if the building is watching the Riesenrad. Inside, rumored to have no right angles; outside, lands on the ground at three different angles. The publicly accessible plaza *underneath* the cantilever is a covered outdoor terrace with concrete benches looking up into the bowed underside, with sightlines to CRAB Studio's red blocks; the campus is open 24/7.
*Sources: architecture, viewpoints*

### WU Campus Ensemble (Welthandelsplatz 1)
Surrounding Hadid's LC are six other buildings by international starchitects, each radically different — CRAB Studio (Peter Cook), Hitoshi Abe, Carme Pinós, BUSarchitektur, NO.MAD. A free, open-air architecture biennial. Walk through in 20 minutes and see six 21st-century languages of building.
*Sources: architecture*

## District 1030 — Landstraße

### Hundertwasserhaus (Kegelgasse 34–38 / Löwengasse 41–43)
Municipal social housing dressed as a Klimt painting (1985). 52 apartments, no straight lines, undulating floors ("an uneven floor is a melody to the feet"), 200 trees rooted in apartments that send branches out the windows as "tree tenants." Hundertwasser refused payment, saying he was just preventing "something ugly being built." It's an actual lived-in building — façade is the only view.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### KunstHaus Wien (Untere Weißgerberstraße 13)
Hundertwasser converted the former Thonet bentwood-furniture factory into a museum of himself (1989–91). Chequerboard façade in black, white and tile-greens; uneven cobblestone under the entrance arcade. The lesser-known Hundertwasser, ten minutes from the famous house — and the only place in Vienna with his permanent gallery.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Haus Wittgenstein (Parkgasse 18)
Designed by Paul Engelmann for Margaret Stonborough-Wittgenstein; her brother Ludwig — fresh off the *Tractatus*, having given away his fortune and tried being a village schoolteacher — joined as co-architect and obsessively redesigned every doorknob, radiator, and window mullion. Engelmann eventually conceded authorship. Pure geometric volumes, no ornament. "Architecture is a gesture." Now the Bulgarian Cultural Institute.
*Sources: architecture, cultural_literary*

### Hofmannsthal apartment (Salesianergasse 12)
The teenage prodigy's parental flat, where he wrote the lyric poetry that astounded Vienna under the pen-name "Loris" before anyone realised he was 17. A spacious late-Biedermeier place — he complained he couldn't concentrate because of his nervous mother and the maids, and eventually fled to Rodaun.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Karl Kraus / Die Fackel office (Hintere Zollamtsstraße 3)
Kraus published 922 issues of *Die Fackel* from this address (1899–1936) — much of it written by him alone, often through the night, dictating in red ink to printers in the same building. He used the magazine to wage thirty years of one-man war on Austrian journalism, hypocrisy, and language itself. No flashy plaque; just the unremarkable façade that housed the loudest voice in the empire.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Rabenhof (Rabengasse 1–3)
A Red Vienna "people's palace" (1925–28) with 1,109 apartments around interlocking courtyards — expressionist brick, jagged setbacks, archways like medieval gatehouses. Built with kindergarten, library, dental clinic, and shared laundry. Architecturally weirder than Karl-Marx-Hof — almost cubist where Karl-Marx-Hof is monumental. Houses the still-active Rabenhof Theater.
*Sources: architecture*

### Belvedere Gardens
The entire baroque garden between Upper and Lower Belvedere is free. Sphinxes — women's heads on lion bodies, symbols of strength + intelligence — flank the upper terrace. Twelve statues of the months line the grand staircase; the Kaskadenbrunnen tumbles down five tiers. Dominique Girard laid it out 1700–1725. The upper-terrace balustrade in front of the Upper Belvedere looks *north* across the parterre, sphinxes, cascades, and Lower Belvedere, with the Stephansdom spire framed dead-centre 1.4 km away — the most composed Baroque-into-Gothic axial view in Vienna.
*Sources: parks_squares, viewpoints*

### Belvedere 21 / 21er Haus (Arsenalstraße 1)
Karl Schwanzer's 1958 Brussels World's Fair pavilion, relocated and re-erected in Vienna — a glass-and-steel building that has crossed a continent. The plaza in front hosts rotating monumental works by Bruno Gironcoli, Erwin Wurm, and Hans Hollein; Wurm's bulging melted forms periodically appear here, on Karlsplatz, and on the Albertina ramp.
*Sources: niche*

### Wien-Mitte public-art axis (Landstraßer Hauptstraße)
The plaza outside Wien-Mitte The Mall (Laurenz Group, 2012) anchors a strip of rotating contemporary commissions — Brigitte Kowanz light works, Eva Schlegel mirrored surfaces, seasonal site-specific pieces — between the station and Hilton Stadtpark.
*Sources: niche*

### Wiener Eislaufverein (Lothringerstraße 22)
The world's largest open-air artificial ice rink (since 1867) — 6,000 m² of ice between the Konzerthaus and Hotel InterContinental, October to March. In summer the same surface hosts open-air cinema and the Vienna Ice Dream concert series. A 19th-century leisure ritual still in production.
*Sources: niche*

### Heizwerk Arsenal (Arsenalstraße 6)
A 1960s district-heating plant whose decorated chimney visible from the Südbahnhof tracks is one of the few brutalist industrial structures left in central Vienna. Supplies hot water to ~40,000 households through underground pipes; the chimney is 90 m tall and has navigation lights for aircraft.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Generali-Turm clock (Landstraßer Hauptstraße 55)
The 1996 office tower's south face carries one of Vienna's largest contemporary public clocks — a clean steel-and-glass dial visible from the Wien Mitte transit hub. Mechanically wound but synchronised by GPS — an engineering split between heritage and modern.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Schweizergarten elevated stone bridge (Landstraßer Gürtel)
A small landscape park between Belvedere and Hauptbahnhof, crossed by a 19th-century stone arch bridge spanning a long-culverted creek bed. The bridge crown is a low GPS-clean elevated spot with sight-lines to Belvedere's Upper Palace, Hauptbahnhof's diamond-roof, and the DC Tower 1 silhouette in the distance.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Südbahnhof traces — Quartier Belvedere (Arsenalstraße / Wiedner Gürtel)
The vast 1873 Südbahnhof terminus was demolished 2009–2010 to make room for the Hauptbahnhof, but its eastern trace remains: the curving Arsenalstraße rail wall, the 1955 rebuilt platforms repurposed as park promenade, the original imperial freight ramp at the southern edge. The Hauptbahnhof's space-frame canopy explicitly does NOT cover the old terminus footprint — the new station respects the ghost.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## District 1040 — Wieden

### Majolikahaus (Linke Wienzeile 40)
Otto Wagner's 1898 apartment block whose entire façade is glazed ceramic tile painted with floral motifs flowing upward like vines climbing the building — invented because Wagner wanted a façade that could be hosed clean. The tile is industrial *Majolika*. Faces the Naschmarkt.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Linke Wienzeile 38 (Medaillonhaus)
Wagner's other Wienzeile house — same year, same block as Majolikahaus, but where its sister has tile, this has Koloman Moser's gold-leaf medallions of stylized muses peering down from beneath palm-frond stucco, and a roof-line crown of stylized female heads in copper. A deliberate pair: one tiled, one gilded.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Naschmarkt (Wienzeile)
"The belly of Vienna." ~130 stalls and eateries built over the now-buried Wienfluss — the market roof you walk past is actually the lid over the river. Saturday flea market is a cult event. Halfway down the western Wienzeile, the two Otto Wagner houses face each other across the stalls.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Theater an der Wien (Linke Wienzeile 6)
Built 1801 by Emanuel Schikaneder — the man who wrote the libretto for Mozart's *Zauberflöte* and originated the role of Papageno. Look for the "Papageno gate" on the side façade: Schikaneder cast in stone as the bird-catcher. Beethoven's *Fidelio* premiered here (1805), and *Die lustige Witwe* exactly a century later (1905).
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Brahms apartment marker (Karlsgasse 4)
Brahms lived on the top floor here from late 1871 until 1897 — his entire late period: the Fourth Symphony, the late piano intermezzi, the Clarinet Quintet. The original building is gone but the address is the same and a marker remains. The Karlskirche dome looms behind it; he saw it from his window every morning.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Third Man Museum exterior (Pressgasse 25)
A fan-built private museum in a Naschmarkt-adjacent ground-floor shopfront — open only Saturdays. The façade carries the iconic zither-and-shadow imagery; passers-by collect at the window even when closed. A whole museum to one film, three streets from where it was shot.
*Sources: niche*

## District 1050 — Margareten

### Reumannhof (Margaretengürtel 100–110)
One of the first major Red Vienna Hofs (1924–26) and a self-described *Gesamtkunstwerk* — architecture, painting, sculpture integrated. The central wing rises like a small tower; flanking wings step down symmetrically. Sits along the Gürtel where U6 trains pass overhead on Wagner's old Stadtbahn viaduct — a Wagner pupil's housing under a Wagner viaduct.
*Sources: architecture*

### Filmcasino (Margaretenstraße 78)
Founded 1911 — one of Vienna's two oldest surviving cinemas. The 1950s remodel left an intact post-war foyer that itself looks like a film set. Programmes lean to international auteur cinema; the building is its own art object.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Margaretenhof (Margaretengürtel 84–86)
A late-1880s Gründerzeit "people's palace" 35 years older than Reumannhof next door — Theophil Hansen-school historicism applied to working-class housing before Red Vienna existed. Brick-and-stucco façade with stepped gables, oriel turrets, and a corner statue of St. Margaret slaying her dragon (the district's namesake). A capitalist-era prototype the socialists later copied at scale a block south.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Schloss Margarethen (Margaretenplatz 2)
A small Renaissance castle (16th-century, rebuilt 1727) hiding inside a courtyard of ordinary apartment buildings — once a country seat of the Habsburgs' chamberlain when this was open land beyond the walls. Above the gate sits a relief of dragon-slaying Saint Margaret; the inner courtyard with its old well is publicly accessible during the day. A pocket castle most Viennese have never seen.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Bettelstudentbrunnen (Margaretenplatz)
A 1929 cast-stone fountain showing the protagonist of Carl Millöcker's operetta *Der Bettelstudent* — the "Beggar Student" — perched on the basin in patched trousers, lyre in hand. Operetta as municipal sculpture: the only fountain in Vienna celebrating a fictional broke musician rather than a saint or a Habsburg.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Hundertwasserbrunnen (Siebenbrunnenplatz)
The "Seven Wells" square once had seven natural springs; the 1846 fountain marks the spot where Vienna's first municipal piped water was tapped before the Alpine pipeline replaced it. Friedensreich Hundertwasser donated a redesign of the surrounds late in life — irregular tile patterns ring the original stone basin.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kaiserstraße Habsburg processional axis
Kaiserstraße was laid out as the Habsburg processional route between the Hofburg and Schönbrunn — Maria Theresa's carriages rolled through here twice daily in summer. The street still runs dead-straight on its original 18th-century survey line; the houses are Biedermeier, the trams are red, and the axis terminates at a palace gate 4 km away.
*Sources: outer_districts*

## District 1060 — Mariahilf

### Köstlergasse 3 (Wagner's own apartment)
The third house of the Wienzeile triumvirate, around the corner — where Wagner himself lived. Less ornamented, more restrained, a hint at the modernism he'd push at Postsparkasse seven years later. The architect's own apartment block: a man designing his own building to walk past on the way to work.
*Sources: architecture*

### Esterházypark (Fritz-Grünbaum-Platz)
A small green pocket in dense Mariahilf, formerly the garden of the Esterházy palace — the noble family who patronized Haydn for three decades at Eisenstadt. The park is a quiet rectangle of plane trees and benches; locals know it as a hidden city living-room.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Café Sperl (Gumpendorfer Straße 11)
Open since 1880 — same Thonet chairs, same marble, same carambole billiard tables. Lehár dropped in regularly while writing operettas at the Theater an der Wien around the corner; the side street is literally called Lehargasse. The Sperl was unofficial HQ of the *Hagenbund* artists' group around 1900.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Haydn-Haus (Haydngasse 19)
Haydn bought this modest suburban house in 1793 and wrote *The Creation* (1796–98) and *The Seasons* (1799–1801) here — an old man inventing modern oratorio in a quiet two-story bourgeois house on what was then the city's edge. Brahms idolised Haydn and walked past this building daily; they were a 10-minute stroll apart.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Mariahilfer Straße pedestrian zone
Vienna's longest commercial promenade, pedestrianized since 2015 after a fierce political referendum. The eastward sight-line ends precisely at Maria-Theresien-Platz — a 19th-century axis aimed at the empress's throne. A street that argues, in stone, that retail is a civic ritual.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Raimund-Brunnen (Wallgasse / Raimundtheater)
A bronze of playwright Ferdinand Raimund as the genie from his own *Der Verschwender*, 1898 — Raimund invented Viennese magic-comedy theatre a generation before Nestroy. The Raimundtheater (1893) sits behind, still staging musicals. A statue of a fictional spirit conjured by a real writer in front of the building named for him.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Theater an der Wien — Papageno side relief (Millöckergasse)
The famous Papagenotor on Wienzeile is matched by a quieter Millöckergasse side façade with the original 1801 stone relief of Schikaneder-as-Papageno surrounded by his three sons — the autograph of the man who commissioned *Die Zauberflöte* and starred in it. The Wienzeile side is the show; this side is the signature.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Capistrankanzel column (Mariahilferstraße / Stiftgasse corner)
Above eye level: a small Baroque column with an angel blowing a trumpet, marking the spot where Franciscan friar John of Capistrano allegedly preached in the 1450s. Vienna has half a dozen of these forgotten preacher-columns — this one is the most overlooked.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Stiftskaserne porch (Mariahilfer Straße 24)
The 1739 Baroque chapel-and-barracks complex by Andrea Altomonte cuts a chunk out of Vienna's main shopping street — soldiers and saints sharing one wall. The chapel façade has gilded military trophies stuck onto the saints' niches: an 18th-century mashup of altar and arsenal.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Fillgraderstiege (Fillgradergasse, off Theobaldgasse)
A Jugendstil staircase by Max Hegele (1907) connecting two streets on Mariahilf's steep edge — wrought-iron lanterns, sinuous curves, lily-of-the-valley balustrades. A miniature Strudlhofstiege most guidebooks miss.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Alfons-Petzold-Hof (Mariahilfer Straße / Andreasgasse)
An unsung 1925 Red Vienna Hof named after a worker-poet who died at 41 of tuberculosis — Petzold wrote socialist verse in a language self-taught from public libraries. The building above the U3 Neubaugasse station displays bronze relief portraits of him under the cornice.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Feuerwache Mariahilf (Mariahilfer Gürtel 1)
A 1970s fire-station whose façade is aluminium-clad sheet metal — a "ship's hull" aesthetic deliberately echoing the period's marine-engineering language. Bay doors oversized for 1970s ladder trucks; today's rigs barely clear them. A piece of post-war infrastructure that hasn't been demolished only because it works.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Café Drechsler (Linke Wienzeile 22)
A 24-hour Vienna institution on the corner of the Naschmarkt — Sir Terence Conran redesigned the interior in 2007 but the exterior is the original 1919 coffee-house façade. Famous as the place that never closes; market porters and night-shift workers share tables at 04:00. Ulrich Seidl filmed here; Falco famously hung around.
*Sources: niche*

### Café Jelinek (Otto-Bauer-Gasse 5)
A wood-panelled 1910 coffee house frequented by Elfriede Jelinek (no relation — coincidence and pun) and used as a literary salon by Mariahilf writers since the 1990s. From outside, easy to miss — a single dark-green door on a quiet side street parallel to Mariahilfer Straße.
*Sources: niche*

## District 1070 — Neubau

### Spittelberg quarter (Spittelberggasse / Schrankgasse / Gutenberggasse)
A grid of cobbled lanes between MuseumsQuartier and Burggasse, lined with pastel Biedermeier houses (1820s–40s) in faded yellow, pink, and green — Vienna's second-largest pedestrian zone. Small "town-square" pockets emerge unexpectedly between the rows. Once a red-light district, now a craft-and-gallery village; Joseph II reportedly visited incognito.
*Sources: architecture, parks_squares, oddities*

### Bellaria Kino (Museumstraße 3)
Founded 1911. Specialises in classic-era films — Marlene Dietrich, Hans Moser, Romy Schneider seasons. Closed 2019, reopened April 2026 after community pressure. The cinema where Vienna goes to see itself on screen.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Hauptbücherei Wien (Urban-Loritz-Platz 2a)
Ernst Mayr's 2003 public library straddles the U6 line on a long stone-clad ramp that doubles as a monumental exterior red staircase rising from the Gürtel like a public stage. The rooftop terrace is free — a café in summer, a parapet promenade in winter, open 9:00–24:00, no library card needed. From the terrace you look east down Mariahilfer Straße toward Maria-Theresien-Platz and west onto Otto Wagner's Gürtel viaducts. People picnic on the steps as if they were the Spanish Steps.
*Sources: viewpoints, niche*

### Volkstheater façade (Neustiftgasse 1)
Fellner & Helmer's 1889 theatre — the same Habsburg-era duo who built theatres in 47 European cities — designed to be "the people's Burgtheater." A relief of muses above the entry, four allegorical figures on the parapet (Tragedy, Comedy, Truth, Beauty). Locals called it *Deutsches Volkstheater* in protest at the elitism of the official Burgtheater.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Volkstheater U-Bahn deep platform
A 1980 U2/U3 station built deep enough to need three escalator banks — the lower platform is 26 metres below street level, the deepest U-Bahn point in central Vienna. The U2 east of here was the city's first fully automated metro segment. The mezzanine is clad in 1970s orange ceramic that is itself a listed period interior.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Siebensternplatz fountain & the Seven Stars
A small triangular square named after a 17th-century house sign showing seven stars — Vienna's Masonic lodge "Zu den sieben Sternen." The modern fountain places seven bronze stars in the cobbles around the basin: a coffee-house Masonic Easter egg.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### MQ "white meadow" balcony (MuseumsQuartier, north court)
On the upper level of the MQ courtyard, a long white concrete balcony cantilevers out over the Burggasse — designed for nothing in particular, no railing on the inner side, just a place to lean and watch. Locals call it *die Wiese* (the meadow). One of the city's best free west-facing sunset spots. Misha Stroj's rotating *Schwebende Steine* installations and the famous PPAG *Enzi* loungers (2002) populate the courtyards below.
*Sources: outer_districts, niche*

### Ulrichsplatz (Sankt-Ulrichs-Platz)
A small triangular Baroque square hidden between Neubaugasse and Burggasse, anchored by the 1721 Ulrichskirche — built on the site of Vienna's oldest documented suburb (founded c. 1211). A village square stranded inside the modern city; the houses around it are mostly 17th–18th century. Walk in and the traffic noise drops.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Amerlinghaus & Schrankgasse 7
A 1715 Biedermeier house with a glassed-in courtyard "Pawlatschen" (open wooden gallery walkways) — Friedrich von Amerling, society portraitist of Sisi's mother, was born here in 1803. One of the last surviving wooden-gallery houses in central Vienna, publicly walk-through.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kirchberggasse "Zur goldenen Krone"
A surviving 17th-century inn sign on an unremarkable apartment block: a gilded crown above the doorway, marking what was once a roadside coaching inn just outside the Stadtmauer. Vienna's house-name system predates street numbers; about 200 medieval signs survive, this is one of the prettiest.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Spittelberg cross-passages (Spittelberggasse ↔ Schrankgasse ↔ Stiftgasse)
Several pastel Biedermeier houses on Spittelberggasse have through-passages connecting parallel cobbled lanes. They turn the Spittelberg grid into a permeable lattice; getting "lost" inside is the point. Once a red-light maze, now a craft-and-café maze with the same geometry.
*Sources: niche*

### Generalihof passage (Mariahilfer Straße 77 → Stiftgasse)
A modernist commercial passage cut through a 1960s Generali building, lined with smaller shops avoiding Mariahilfer Straße rents. A late example of the Vienna Durchhaus tradition recast in chrome and travertine.
*Sources: niche*

### Burggasse-Stadthalle Stadtbahn station (Burggasse 113 / Urban-Loritz-Platz)
Otto Wagner's surviving Stadtbahn station entry pavilion (1898) for the Gürtel viaduct line — apple-green ironwork, tiled half-dome, sunflower wreaths along the gable. Now a U6 stop where commuters pour past Wagner's last great pavilion every 90 seconds without looking up.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Gürtelbögen — Stadtbahn arches (along U6 viaduct, Heiligenstadt → Westbahnhof)
Wagner's 1898 Stadtbahn viaduct along the Gürtel was deliberately built with 48+ deep brick arches as ground-floor commercial space — a piece of 19th-century mixed-use engineering. Today the arches are a 4-km strip of bars, clubs, galleries, repair shops. Walk underneath at night and the viaduct functions exactly as Wagner specified, 130 years on.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Hofmobiliendepot exterior (Andreasgasse 7)
The imperial furniture warehouse of the Habsburgs — a yellow-stuccoed neo-Renaissance block on a quiet Neubau side street where the Habsburgs literally stored their spare thrones, beds, and parade carriages. Built 1747, expanded 1900. The street façade hides a stockpile that once supplied every Habsburg residence in the empire.
*Sources: niche*

## District 1080 — Josefstadt

### Palais Auersperg gardens (Auerspergstraße 1)
Baroque palace where the 7-year-old Mozart played his first Vienna concert (1762). The forecourt and side gardens are accessible from the street — a quiet pocket between the busy Ring and the Josefstadt theaters, often missed because it doesn't look like a public space.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Stefan Zweig apartment (Kochgasse 8)
Zweig lived here for the twelve years that made his name (1907–1919): early *Sternstunden der Menschheit*, the Verlaine and Verhaeren translations, the friendship with Romain Rolland, the war. The 1962 plaque says only: "This house was from 1907–1919 home and place of work of the poet Stefan Zweig (1881–1942)." Tight, factual, almost defiant — the same prose register as Zweig himself.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Piaristenkirche & Jodok-Fink-Platz
Lukas von Hildebrandt's 1716 Baroque church on a quiet cobbled square — twin onion-domed towers, Maulbertsch frescoes inside. Anton Bruckner played the organ here. The square outside is a hidden village green with chestnut trees and a Marian column; almost no tourists find it. One of Vienna's most photogenic Baroque ensembles.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Theater in der Josefstadt (Josefstädter Straße 26)
Vienna's oldest continuously-operating theatre (1788) — Beethoven conducted his *Weihe des Hauses* overture at the reopening on 3 October 1822, the only opening of any theatre Beethoven ever conducted. Max Reinhardt redesigned the interior in 1924 and ran it until 1937. The chandelier still dims by being slowly lowered into the ceiling, a Reinhardt invention.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Schönborn Park (Laudongasse 15–19)
The former private garden of Palais Schönborn (now the Volkskundemuseum) — Hildebrandt designed the palace in 1706 for an Imperial Vice-Chancellor. The park is a flat green rectangle with old-growth plane trees, gravel paths and a single Baroque garden pavilion. Free; locals use it as their living room.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Lange Gasse — Beethoven trail
Beethoven moved 68 times in 35 years in Vienna and Lange Gasse claims at least four addresses — Nr. 26 (1815), Nr. 46 (1821), Nr. 70. The whole 1.2-km Biedermeier corridor is a string of plaques and faded house numbers tracking his serial relocation. Walk it and you're tracing a 19th-century music gypsy.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Strozzigasse villas
A short street running uphill toward the Rathaus — the houses are Biedermeier-to-Gründerzeit with elaborate stucco caryatids and Atlantes holding up bay windows. The 1880s building boom rolled through here when industrialists wanted addresses close to the Ring but cheaper than inside it. A free Gründerzeit façade museum.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Skodagasse arcade
A short curving street named after surgeon Joseph Skoda (the "Skoda phenomenon" in chest medicine is named for his percussion technique). Lined with 1880s pharmacies and medical-instrument shops — the historic apothecary's quarter of Vienna's medical district.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Hamerlingpark (Hamerlingplatz)
A small landscaped square named for poet Robert Hamerling, with a 1900 bronze bust on a Jugendstil pedestal at the center and curved gravel paths. The park is barely bigger than a tennis court but functions as the social hinge of the Josefstadt — terrace cafés spill out around it.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Alser Straße — Trinitarian Order corner
At the Lange Gasse / Alser Straße corner stands a Baroque relief of the Holy Trinity above a former monastery gate — the Trinitarian Order ransomed Christian slaves from North African pirates in the 17th–18th centuries, an unusual order whose Vienna monastery operated a public ransom collection box here. The relief survives; the function does not.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Engerthbrunnen (Rathauspark NW corner)
A modest 1899 fountain dedicated to Wilhelm von Engerth, the railway engineer who built the Semmering line — the world's first mountain railway and a UNESCO site. A small Symbolist bronze of a steam locomotive emerging from rock; an engineer's monument shaped like the engineering.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Loquai-Park sphinxes (Friedrich-Schmidt-Platz)
A small park behind the Rathaus with two surviving Baroque garden sphinxes — relocated from a demolished Josefstadt palais in the 1890s. They face each other across a path that no one walks; one of the city's quietest "found objects."
*Sources: niche*

### Justizpalast Justizcafé rooftop (Schmerlingplatz 11)
The 1881 Justice Ministry hides a publicly accessible café-terrace on its top floor — security check at the door, then an elevator to a glass-roofed atrium and an outdoor balustrade looking out over Rathaus, Burgtheater, and Parliament. Mon–Fri ~07:30–17:00, free if you sign the visitor register. Vienna's least-known central rooftop view.
*Sources: viewpoints*

## District 1090 — Alsergrund

### Berggasse 19 (Freud's address)
Sigmund Freud lived and saw patients here for 47 years (1891–1938). *Die Traumdeutung*, the Wolf Man, the Rat Man, Anna O., the founding of psychoanalysis — all from this address. The couch left with him for London in 1938. The street door, staircase, and brass plate are visible from the public pavement.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Strudlhofstiege (Strudlhofgasse / Liechtensteinstraße)
A 1910 Jugendstil outdoor staircase in luminescent Mannersdorf limestone by Theodor Jaeger, with terraces, fountains and lamps. Heimito von Doderer made it the centrepiece of his 900-page 1951 novel *Die Strudlhofstiege* — his opening poem is mounted at the foot of the stairs. A staircase that became literature.
*Sources: oddities, cultural_literary*

### Vienna Circle plaque (Boltzmanngasse 5)
The Thursday-evening seminar of the Wiener Kreis (Schlick, Carnap, Neurath, Hahn, Gödel) met in a ground-floor classroom of the Institute of Physics from 1924 to 1936 — and reinvented philosophy from there. A plaque on the façade marks the room. The street itself is named for Boltzmann, whose entropy formula is engraved on his Arkadenhof bust nearby.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Liechtensteinpark (Fürstengasse 1)
A baroque private park opened to the public daily 7:15–20:30. Once one of Vienna's three great baroque gardens (with Belvedere and Augarten); now a quiet, almost-forgotten green rectangle in the Rossau. The Palais inside houses Rubens, Rembrandt, Raphael — visible only by booking — but the park is free.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Servitenplatz (Servitengasse / Grünentorgasse)
Vienna's "Little Paris": a paved square in front of the 17th-century baroque Servitenkirche, ringed by florists, French bistros, and one of the city's quietest pre-Reformation churches. The fountain, cobbled platz, and village feel sit improbably ten minutes from the Ring.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Spittelau Incinerator (Spittelauer Lände 45)
A working waste incinerator Hundertwasser redressed after a 1987 fire (1988–92). Golden onion-dome on a smokestack, irregular windows, mosaic skin, rooftop trees. Burns ~250,000 tons of trash a year and powers district heating for 60,000 homes. The most aesthetic-correct industrial structure on earth — visible from the U6/U4 platform at Spittelau.
*Sources: architecture*

### Otto Wagner Schützenhaus (Obere Donaustraße, Donaukanal)
A miniature Jugendstil pavilion sitting on the Donaukanal as part of the never-fully-built canal lock system (1904–08) — blue ceramic-tile waves run along its parapet. Originally a sluice control house; now a restaurant that looks like a tiled blue-and-white biscuit tin floated up and beached itself on the embankment.
*Sources: architecture*

### Schubert Geburtshaus (Nußdorfer Straße 54)
A modest two-story Biedermeier survivor on a busy commercial street — Schubert was born here on 31 January 1797, the schoolmaster's son in a family of fourteen children crammed into a "Pawlatschenhaus" courtyard arrangement. The plaque is small; the contrast with what came out of this address (six hundred Lieder) is the whole point.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Hundekot-Roboter relic (Sigmund-Freud-Park)
In 1992 Vienna deployed yellow vacuum-suction "WC-Watschler" robots to suck up dog excrement from sidewalks — a globally mocked experiment retired within two years. A handful of original yellow *"Bitte gegen den Rinnstein"* dog-curb signs survive embedded in Sigmund-Freud-Park's pathways: a fossil layer of municipal absurdity.
*Sources: niche*

### Franz-Josefs-Bahnhof (Julius-Tandler-Platz)
The least monumental of Vienna's terminus stations — a stripped 1970s rebuild over the 1872 original platforms. The interior columns and the curved track shed are still in use; from the platform end the line vanishes northwest toward Tulln and Český Velenice. Often forgotten because it serves only regional routes.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## District 1100 — Favoriten

### Wien Hauptbahnhof (Am Hauptbahnhof 1)
14 diamond-shaped space-frame canopies arranged in five rows, deliberately staggered to zigzag light onto the platforms — best seen from the public bridge that crosses over the tracks. Pale, faceted, crystalline; deliberately not symmetric. The roof is the building.
*Sources: architecture*

### Reumannplatz & Tichy ice cream
The square named after Vienna's first socialist mayor (Jakob Reumann, elected 1919) — anchor of working-class Favoriten. The 1980 marble fountain by Anton Hanak shows nude figures striving upward toward a sun; the 1953 Tichy ice cream shop on the corner invented the *Eismarillenknödel* (apricot dumpling reimagined as ice cream) that Viennese still queue for.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Viktor-Adler-Markt (Viktor-Adler-Platz)
Vienna's most Anatolian street market — börek, Balkan cheese, Turkish bridal-veil shops, no tourists. Named for the founder of Austrian social democracy. The 1899 market hall has cast-iron pillars and an arched glass roof; the open-air stalls spread along Quellenstraße. Saturday mornings are the moment.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Per-Albin-Hansson-Siedlung
A 1947–51 garden-suburb of single-family terraced houses on the southern fringe — postwar utopia built on the Swedish *folkhemmet* model, named for the Swedish PM whose welfare-state writings inspired it. Low pitched roofs, garden plots, white-rendered walls, a single church at the center. A working-class arcadia 20 minutes from Stephansplatz.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Böhmischer Prater (Laaer-Berg-Straße)
A miniature wooden funfair tucked into the south-eastern hills, founded 1882 by Bohemian brick-workers who needed weekend amusement after their shifts. Has a 1909 carved-horse carousel still in operation, a wooden swing-ride from 1900, and Kreidl's beer garden. The world's smallest still-functioning Belle Époque amusement park.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wasserturm Favoriten (Windtenstraße 3)
A 67-metre brick water tower (1899) by Franz Borkowitz — Romantic-historicist with corner turrets, copper roof, and a 1,000-cubic-metre tank near the top. Decommissioned 1910, kept as a listed monument because by then it had become an unmistakable Favoriten landmark. A piece of Victorian industrial decoration disguised as a fortress; open to climb on select summer Sundays.
*Sources: infrastructure, outer_districts*

### Wasserturm Wienerberg (Triester Straße / Hertha-Firnberg-Straße)
A 1898 cylindrical brick tower that supplied the Wienerberg brickworks — the same factory that produced most of Vienna's Gründerzeit Ringstrasse buildings. The tower outlived the works (closed 1968); now a freestanding monument in a park-and-residential redevelopment, surrounded by 21st-century office towers it pre-dates by a century.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Wienerberg — Wienerblick lookout (Wienerbergstraße)
A 70 m artificial landscape hill built from 1970s construction rubble at the southern edge of Vienna, capped by a small wooden viewing platform installed in 2010. From the top, the city's high-rise corridor (DC Tower 1, Donauturm, the Wienerberg towers themselves) is visible as a north-south spine. Free, reachable by bus 7A from Reumannplatz U1.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### George-Washington-Hof (Wienerbergstraße 16–20)
A 1.6-km-long inter-war municipal housing complex (1927–30) named for the U.S. president — a deliberate Red Vienna nod to American democracy. Built in pavilion-style with vast open gardens between blocks (1,085 apartments, 50% green space) — the anti-Karl-Marx-Hof, low-rise where Ehn went high.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Quellenplatz Marian column
A 1721 Baroque Marian column transplanted to Quellenplatz from an inner suburb during the 1890s — Mary on a spiral pillar surrounded by four saints (Roch, Sebastian, Charles Borromeo, Rosalia). Sits stranded in a small triangular square in the heart of Anatolian Favoriten, conducting a continuous and silent religious dialogue with the neighborhood.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Umspannwerk Favoriten (Humboldtplatz)
A 1929 Wien Energie electrical substation by Eugen Kastner and Fritz Waage in stripped neoclassical brick — towers and high windows that look almost ecclesiastical. Still steps down 110 kV to 20 kV for the south Vienna grid. A power-cathedral hiding in plain sight on a leafy square.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## District 1110 — Simmering

### Gasometer City (Guglgasse 6–14)
Four 19th-century brick gas-storage cylinders, each ~70 m tall, gutted in the 1990s and given new internal architecture by four different starchitects (Nouvel, Coop Himmelb(l)au, Wehdorn, Holzbauer). Each cylinder is a vertical mini-city. From U3 Gasometer station the four look like fortified silos from a planet that runs on bricks; each interior is radically different.
*Sources: architecture*

### Neugebäude (Otmar-Brix-Gasse 1)
A massive 16th-century Renaissance imperial palace ruin on the eastern edge of Simmering — Maximilian II built it 1569–1576 on the spot where the Ottoman sultan's tent stood during the 1529 siege, as a deliberate "we took your camp" gesture. Long arcaded courtyard, ruined twin towers, slowly being restored. The most enormous building in Vienna almost nobody knows exists.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kaiser-Ebersdorf Schloss (Münnichplatz)
A 13th-century moated castle that Maximilian I used as a hunting lodge — Charles V slept here on his way to the Diet of Worms in 1521. Now mostly hidden behind a 19th-century prison wall (still a working prison), but the gatehouse, drawbridge moat-line and one tower are visible from the public street. A medieval castle that became a state penitentiary by historical accident.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Geiselberg watertower & viewpoint
The Geiselberg is a small rise on the Simmeringer Heide with a 1910 brick water tower at its summit — climb the public path for one of the most underrated panoramas in Vienna's east, with the refinery flares of Schwechat on the horizon.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Simmeringer Heide
An unmowed steppe-grassland remnant on Vienna's south-east edge — the last fragment of the pre-urban Pannonian dry grassland that once covered the whole eastern basin. Protected since 1992; in May it's purple with sage and milkvetch. A piece of the Hungarian Puszta inside Vienna city limits.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wasserturm Simmering (Kaiser-Ebersdorfer Straße)
The 1873 octagonal brick water tower for the southeast Vienna network — supplied by the *First Vienna Mountain Spring Pipeline*, the 95-km gravity-fed aqueduct from the Schneeberg Alps to the city. The tower no longer holds water; it stands as a memorial to the 1873 engineering miracle that gave Vienna some of Europe's best tap-water.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Liesing-Inzersdorf high-voltage gate (Kledering edge)
At the southeast edge of the city the 380 kV transmission lines from the Slovak grid first enter Vienna's distribution network — a fenced electrical compound the size of two football fields. From the Liesingbach cycle path you see the porcelain insulators stretching toward Bratislava. The hum is constant.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## District 1120 — Meidling

### Theresienbad (Hufelandgasse 3)
A Jugendstil public bath (1898, rebuilt 1956 after wartime damage) on the site of a 1750s mineral spring discovered when Maria Theresa was empress — hence the name. The exterior keeps the 1898 turret and tile work; the interior is mid-century modern. Vienna's oldest continuously-operating public swimming pool.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Meidlinger Hauptstraße pedestrian zone
A 600-metre Gründerzeit shopping street pedestrianised in 1989 — Vienna's first outside-the-Ring pedestrian zone. The 1880s tenements still have their stucco angels and shop signs; the southern end terminates at the Bezirksamt with a small Jugendstil clock tower. The "small Mariahilfer Straße" for locals who can't afford it.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Tivoligasse / Edelsitz axis
A narrow lane along the western edge of Schönbrunn Park, lined with Biedermeier "Edelsitze" (noble country seats) from when Meidling was Habsburg dacha country — small palaces with garden walls, wisteria and cracked stucco coats of arms over the gates. Walk it at dusk for the imperial-twilight feel.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Phorus-Park (Phorusplatz)
A pocket park named after the constellation Argo Navis's prow ("Phorus") because the surrounding streets were named in the 1880s after astronomical features — Sternwartepark, Tirolergasse, Aichholzgasse make a 19th-century astronomy district. A 1900 cast-iron bandstand sits at the center.
*Sources: outer_districts*

## District 1130 — Hietzing

### Schönbrunn — Neptunbrunnen
Franz Anton von Zauner's 1780 fountain. The sea-goddess Thetis kneels before Neptune, asking safe voyage for her son Achilles to Troy. Maria Theresia commissioned it; the pool frames the long axial walk back to the palace — a deliberate Baroque optical trick. The stone balustrade landing on the climb up to the Gloriette is the spot where seahorses, parterre, palace, and the Stephansdom spire (6 km away on the city horizon) all line up in a single composed Baroque axis.
*Sources: parks_squares, viewpoints*

### Schönbrunn — Tirolerhof meadow (west park)
A reconstructed Alpine farmhouse and meadow on the western edge of Schönbrunn's free zone, with a small rise giving a side-view of the Gloriette from below the formal axis — the only place in the park where the Gloriette is framed by Tyrolean hay barns instead of clipped hornbeam. Free entry, often empty.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Schönbrunn — Maze hill exterior
The Irrgarten (maze) charges admission, but the surrounding grass mound — a deliberate 19th-century earthwork meant to be looked down on from the Gloriette — is freely accessible from the perimeter path. Standing on the mound, you see the maze pattern from above without paying.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Tiergarten Schönbrunn entrance gate (Maxingstraße 13b)
The 1752 wrought-iron Rococo gates of the world's oldest continuously-operating zoo, founded by Franz Stephan as a menagerie for Maria Theresia's breakfast pavilion. The entrance pavilions and the perimeter wall — visible from Maxingstraße — are the original baroque structures; you don't need a ticket to see the gates or the imperial coat of arms above them.
*Sources: niche*

### Hietzinger Brücke — Wienfluss portal
Where the Wien river crosses out of Schönbrunn and dives underground heading east, Otto Wagner's Stadtbahn engineering team built a granite-faced portal arch (1898) with Secessionist iron grilles — practically invisible from the bridge above. Stand on the bridge, lean over, and the river vanishes into Vienna's most beautiful storm drain.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Klimt Villa (Feldmühlgasse 11)
Klimt's last studio (1911–1918) in a Hietzing garden villa, where he painted *The Bride* and the late portraits. The garden gate, façade, and small front garden are visible from the public street — a low yellow-and-white villa hiding behind a hedge in a residential lane.
*Sources: niche*

### Lainzer Tor (Hermesstraße / Lainzer Tor)
The principal gateway into the Lainzer Tiergarten — a 1782 Baroque iron-and-stone gatehouse with the imperial double eagle still mounted on the keystone. The wall runs 22 km around the reserve, enclosing it since Ferdinand I in 1561. From outside the gate, the wall just goes on forever in both directions.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Roter Berg viewing tower (St.-Veit-Gasse)
A small 1898 wooden observation tower on the Roter Berg hill — restored 2010, free to climb. Views over Hietzing, Schönbrunn's Gloriette, and the Wienerwald. The hill itself is a former vineyard; old stone wine-press foundations are scattered around the path.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Schloss Wolkersbergen (Hütteldorf)
A 1718 Baroque country house on the Wolkersberg in upper Hietzing — yellow stucco, hipped roof, surrounded by a garden the city now keeps semi-public. The view down into Hütteldorf and across the Wien valley is what Joseph Roth described as the "real countryside ten minutes from the Hofburg."
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Otto Wagner Wientallinie viaducts (U4 Hütteldorf → Karlsplatz)
The U4 from Hütteldorf to Karlsplatz runs on Wagner's 1894–1899 Stadtbahn viaducts — a continuous 13-km Jugendstil railway built in five years by 8,000 workers. Olive-green ironwork, granite-block piers, glazed brick tunnels, Schimkowitz-style station heads. The longest continuously preserved Jugendstil engineering work in Europe; you ride through it for €2.40.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Schönbrunn — Roman Ruins (faux Carthage)
Fake ruins built 1778 by Hetzendorf von Hohenberg, deliberately crumbling, as Enlightenment landscape philosophy: a meditation on the transience of empires. Real Roman spolia were embedded in the artificial walls to make it more "authentic."
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Schönbrunn — Obelisk Fountain
A 1777 stone obelisk with fake hieroglyphs — the symbols are pure ornament, not real Egyptian writing. Sits on a grotto of river gods. Egyptomania pre-dating Napoleon's expedition by twenty years.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Schönbrunn — Gloriette
A 30-metre triumphal arch built 1775 to celebrate the Habsburg victory at Kolín (1757) — which they actually partly lost. Purely decorative, structurally useless, possibly the most Habsburg object in Vienna: monumental, ornate, and there only to be looked at from across the gardens. The climb up the hill is free.
*Sources: parks_squares, oddities*

### Otto Wagner Hofpavillon Hietzing
A private U-Bahn station Otto Wagner built for one man — Emperor Franz Joseph — who used it exactly twice in his life. Copper-domed waiting room with mahogany paneling and a Carl Moll bird's-eye painting of Vienna seen from the Gloriette. Functionally redundant from the moment it was built: pure architectural sycophancy.
*Sources: architecture*

### Haus Steiner (St.-Veit-Gasse 10)
Loos's most cited domestic work (1910), built the same year as the Looshaus. A vaulted barrel-roof on the street side (zoning forbade more than one storey street-facing); the garden façade is a flat, three-storey modernist box. Two utterly different buildings depending on which side you stand on.
*Sources: architecture*

### Haus Scheu (Larochegasse 3)
The first stepped-terrace house in continental Europe (Loos, 1912) — each storey set back from the one below, creating roof gardens on top of each level. Neighbours petitioned against it ("looks like Algiers!" was a literal complaint). Now an unremarkable suburban house from the street; the radicalism is in the section, not the elevation.
*Sources: architecture*

### Haus Strasser (Kupelwiesergasse 28)
Loos retrofit (1918–19) of an existing house — significant because by 1919 Loos had developed the *Raumplan*: rooms of different heights interlocking in three dimensions. The street façade looks almost banal; the radicalism is what's hidden inside.
*Sources: architecture*

### Werkbundsiedlung Wien (Veitingergasse / Woinovichgasse)
A model housing settlement of 70 small houses by 32 architects (1930–32) — Vienna's answer to Stuttgart's Weissenhof. Loos contributed two, Richard Neutra one, Gerrit Rietveld two. A complete open-air gallery of 1930s European modernism, all still inhabited as private homes.
*Sources: architecture*

### Hermesvilla (Lainzer Tiergarten)
Franz Joseph commissioned this "Palace of Dreams" by Hasenauer (1882–86) to keep the perpetually-restless Sisi in Vienna. The villa's name comes from the garden statue of Hermes — messenger god, traveler, restlessness incarnate, basically Sisi's patron saint. Klimt and Matsch decorated the interiors. She got an entire forested nature reserve and still wouldn't stay.
*Sources: statues_monuments*

### Lainzer Tiergarten
A 24.5 km² walled hunting reserve enclosed by Ferdinand I in 1561, opened to the public only in 1919. Today: 800–1,000 wild boars roam free, plus mouflons, fallow deer, and red deer. Seven gates with different schedules. Free entry.
*Sources: parks_squares*

## District 1140 — Penzing

### Kirche am Steinhof (Baumgartner Höhe 1)
Otto Wagner's golden-domed Jugendstil church (1903–07), perched on a hill in what was Vienna's psychiatric hospital. Wagner designed it specifically for patients: rounded corners, flowing-water fonts, separate doors for men and women, emergency exits behind the altar, almost no crucifixes. White Carrara marble panels held on by exposed copper rivets; gilded copper dome catches sun for miles; four golden-winged angels by Schimkowitz above the entry. Architecture as therapy. The south-facing terrace below the church is a separate experience: free public path, broad city panorama framed by the dome rising behind you. Behind the church, the old Pulverturm — a circular 1840s stone powder magazine — sits on a forest clearing 40 m higher than the church terrace, the dome below you rather than above.
*Sources: architecture, oddities, viewpoints*

### Otto-Wagner-Spital pavilions (Baumgartner Höhe)
Around Wagner's golden-domed church extends a 144-hectare campus of 60 identical pavilions (1904–1907) — Carlo von Boog's masterpiece of "pavilion psychiatry," each ward a free-standing yellow Jugendstil block in a forest setting. Wandering the public paths is wandering the world's largest preserved Belle Époque hospital landscape.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Jubiläumswarte (Johann-Staud-Straße)
A slim 31 m white-painted concrete observation tower (1898 wooden original, rebuilt 1956) on the 449 m Mostalm above Ottakring/Penzing — Vienna's highest free climbable lookout tower, open weekends April–October, free admission. 360-degree panorama: Stephansdom east, Wienerwald west, Schneeberg on a clear day. Bus 46B + 15-minute forest walk.
*Sources: viewpoints, outer_districts*

### Hadikgasse villa quarter (Hadikgasse 28+)
Where Hietzinger Hauptstraße becomes Hadikgasse runs a strip of 1880s villas built for Imperial-era civil servants who wanted to walk to Schönbrunn but live cheaper than Hietzing. The houses have wrought-iron garden gates, faded family monograms, and one or two surviving carriage barns. A bourgeois fossil belt.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Technisches Museum exterior (Mariahilfer Straße 212)
The 1909 neo-classical museum building — Emperor Franz Joseph laid the cornerstone for his diamond jubilee. Exterior is freely walk-around: stone reliefs of inventors above the entry (Werner Siemens, Carl Auer von Welsbach, Ressel — the propeller engineer also Vienna-buried), and a restored Stephanie-Warte original wrought-iron observation tower on the grounds.
*Sources: outer_districts*

## District 1150 — Rudolfsheim-Fünfhaus

### Westbahnhof (Europaplatz 2/3)
Vienna's first major post-war public building (Robert Hartinger, 1949–52) — a clean modernist replacement for the bombed 1858 imperial terminus, with a cantilevered concrete canopy over the entry, a freestanding clock tower, and Joseph Humplik's relief "Help in Need" / "Spirit of Travel" in the entry hall. Around the corner, a few surviving cast-iron columns of the 1858 imperial station are still in use in the underground tracks — visible from the U6 platform. The original 1950s mosaic floor pattern survives.
*Sources: infrastructure, outer_districts*

### Reindorfgasse art-street
A narrow 1-km Gründerzeit street that the 2010s turned into Vienna's most concentrated street-art corridor — every other shutter painted, semi-curated by the local "Reindorfgassenfest" community festival. The houses are 1880s-bourgeois plain; the layer on top is contemporary graffiti. The walk happens entirely outdoors.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Märzpark (Mariahilfer Gürtel)
A 5-hectare park named after the March 1848 revolution that briefly toppled Metternich. Carved into the park's edge sits a stone memorial of the 1848 barricades. The park borders the Stadthalle and connects to the Schmelz via a footbridge — a green hinge between western working-class districts.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Storchengasse — Storchenhaus relief
A 19th-century apartment building with a large stork relief over the entry — the bird carrying a swaddled baby in its beak, a 1900s natalist motif. The street is named after the bird, the house quotes the street's name, and the relief explains both: a self-referential Vienna street.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wiener Stadthalle (Roland-Rainer-Platz 1 / Vogelweidplatz 14)
Roland Rainer's 1958 multi-purpose hall — Austria's largest indoor arena, built on hyperbolic-paraboloid roof geometry. The cantilevered concrete forms over the entrance and the segmented main hall are pure Cold-War-era continental modernism, suspended from V-shaped piers. Free to walk around any time outside event hours.
*Sources: niche, outer_districts*

### Maria-vom-Siege-Kirche (Mariahilfer Gürtel 76)
A vast 1875 neo-Romanesque pilgrimage church with a 76-metre dome — Friedrich von Schmidt (the Rathaus architect) designed it as a memorial to the relief of Vienna in 1683. Stands isolated on a traffic island where Mariahilfer Gürtel meets the Gürtel: the most dramatically situated church in the western districts, almost no tourists visit.
*Sources: outer_districts*

## District 1160 — Ottakring

### Sandleitenhof (Sandleitengasse / Rosa-Luxemburg-Gasse)
Vienna's largest single Red Vienna housing complex: 1,587 apartments across multiple courtyards, designed 1924–28 as a self-sufficient neighborhood with schools, library, theater, public bath, and shops. Often called "the proletarian Versailles." Layout is axial, almost Baroque — democracy borrowing the visual grammar of monarchy on purpose.
*Sources: architecture*

### Sandleitenkapelle (Liebknechtgasse / Sandleitenhof)
A tiny 1928 Red Vienna chapel folded inside the Sandleitenhof complex — a socialist housing project building a worker's chapel was a contested act (the SDAP was militantly secular). The compromise: a stripped-down expressionist brick chapel with no spire, just a cross above the door. Architecturally unique in Vienna.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kongresspark
A large park between Ottakringer Straße and the Sandleitenhof — created 1928 with a vast central rose garden, a 1930 children's wading pool (still working), and bronze allegorical statues of "Work" and "Family" by Anton Hanak that look more like Soviet-realist propaganda than Red Vienna. Wedding photos every Saturday.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Ottakringer Brauerei (Ottakringer Platz 1)
Vienna's only big brewery still inside city limits — founded 1837, brick-and-iron Gründerzeit production halls, public brewery yard accessible during opening hours. The 35-metre red brick chimney is an Ottakring skyline marker; Friday afternoon Biergarten opens into the courtyard among the copper kettles.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wilhelminenberg vineyards
Vienna is one of the few capitals with commercial vineyards inside city limits — the Wilhelminenberg slope, west of Ottakring, has been planted since the 12th century. Public footpaths run between the rows; the Heurigen at the top serve the wine pressed 50 metres downhill. A working vineyard a tram-ride from the inner city.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wilhelminenberg-Warte / Habsburgwarte (Galitzinberg, Savoyenstraße 2)
A 23 m granite-block lookout tower (1888) on the 388 m summit of the Galitzinberg, built by the Austrian Tourist Club for Empress Elisabeth's birthday and dedicated to the Habsburgs — five storeys of cut stone, free to climb. Panorama from Bratislava to the Schneeberg. Surrounded by free-roaming horse paddocks of the adjacent Schloss Wilhelminenberg. Bus 46B from Ottakring U3, then a 12-minute forest path.
*Sources: viewpoints, outer_districts*

### Schloss Wilhelminenberg terrace (Savoyenstraße 2)
A 1908 hilltop villa-castle built for Archduke Leopold Salvator at 348 m, with a south-facing balustrade terrace looking down on all of Vienna — the schloss is now a hotel, but the front terrace and gravel forecourt are publicly accessible by paved access road.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Maurerwaldweg ridgeline overlooks (Wilhelminenberg → Schafberg)
A 4 km forested ridge between the two western lookout hills, with three small unmarked grass clearings facing east over the city — locals call them *die Schauplätze*. Each clearing carries an old wooden bench, no signage, no commerce. Free GPS-friendly openings in dense beech canopy.
*Sources: viewpoints*

## District 1170 — Hernals

### Kalvarienberg-Kirche (Kalvarienberggasse 23)
A 1700 pilgrimage church on Hernals's only hill, with 17 outdoor chapels (the *Kreuzweg*) lining the path up the slope — each a small Baroque shrine with painted relief. Used to host the largest Easter pilgrimage in Vienna; now a quiet ascent through a stations-of-the-cross arranged like a sculpture garden. Free.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Hernalser Hauptstraße — Mädchen mit Korb relief
At Hernalser Hauptstraße 78, a high-relief sculpture of a girl with a basket marks an 1880s laundry — the Hernals stream once powered washing operations for the whole western city. A small commercial sign that became sculpture, then survived its trade by a century.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Elterleinplatz fountain
The geographical center of Hernals — a triangular square with a 1909 cast-iron drinking fountain that still works in summer (Vienna's free public-fountain network has ~900 of these). The square is named after a 17th-century mayor; the surrounding façades are pure 1890s Gründerzeit.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Schwarzenbergpark (Neuwaldegg)
The largest English landscape park in Vienna (1765), at Hernals's western edge — owned originally by the Schwarzenberg family, donated to the city 1958. Allées of beech, a Doric "temple of friendship," a serpentine stream. The entry gates on Waldeggstraße are wrought-iron 18th-century survivors.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Heiliger Mathias Kirche corner (Veronikagasse)
A 1910 brick parish church by Karl Holey with a 50-metre tower visible across the district. The chestnut-shaded square in front holds a Madonna column (1726) transplanted from the inner city when the church was built — a 200-year time shuffle in one small space.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Schneekugelmanufaktur Perzy (Schumanngasse 87)
The world's first snow-globe was invented here by surgical-instrument maker Erwin Perzy in 1900 while trying to amplify a lightbulb — the snow was semolina. The original family workshop still operates on Schumanngasse; the modest factory façade is unmarked except for a small sign. A 125-year-old Vienna invention still made by hand at the original address.
*Sources: niche*

### Exelberg ridge & Tulbingerkogel sightline
The Exelberg (514 m) carries Vienna's main FM broadcasting station — the fenced antenna compound is closed, but the surrounding ridge trail has a stone seating-bench placed in 1976 looking north toward the Tulbingerkogel and the lower Wachau. The least urban of all Vienna lookout points; on a clear winter day the Schneeberg and Rax peaks are visible on the southern horizon behind you.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Dreimarkstein clearing (1170/1190 border)
A small forest hilltop between Hermannskogel and Heuberg (456 m), named for the three boundary stones that once marked the meeting of Vienna's old municipal districts. The 1750s engraved stones are still in place, half-buried in moss; the clearing opens south over the city. A 25-minute walk from Salmannsdorf bus terminus (35A).
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Brunnenmarkt + Yppenplatz (Brunnengasse)
Vienna's longest street market — 170+ stalls of Turkish spice, Balkan börek, Austrian root vegetables. Cheaper, louder, less touristed than Naschmarkt. At the north end the 1880s Yppenmarkt hall opens onto Yppenplatz, the brunch-and-natural-wine plaza where Yugoslavs, Turks, Austrians and the 2010s creative class share one set of café tables. Saturday morning is the social peak.
*Sources: parks_squares, outer_districts*

## District 1180 — Währing

### Stadtbahn Pavilion Volksoper (Währinger Straße)
A surviving original Otto Wagner Stadtbahn station entry (1896–98), less restored and grainier than Karlsplatz — still in active U6 use. The U6 line runs on Wagner's original Vorortelinie viaduct, so every ride on this stretch crosses brick arches Wagner designed in the 1890s.
*Sources: architecture*

### Türkenschanzpark — Paulinenwarte tower
A 31-metre sandstone observation tower (1888) named after Princess Pauline Metternich, who championed the park's creation. The "Türkenschanze" itself refers to the 1683 Ottoman siege earthworks that once stood here — the park is a former battlefield, now buried under labeled trees from China, Japan, and North America.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Türkenschanzpark — waterfalls & Yunus Emre Fountain
Over 6,500 m² of water surfaces: cascading streams, ponds, a small waterfall. The Yunus Emre Fountain (donated by the Turkish embassy in 1991) honors the 13th-century Sufi mystic-poet — a peaceful gesture set on top of an old Ottoman battlefield. Botanical labels everywhere make the park a free open-air encyclopedia. The upper meadow on the southern slope is the highest landscaped point inside the Gürtel (~210 m), with Karlskirche dome and Donauturm both visible in one panning sweep.
*Sources: parks_squares, viewpoints*

### Pötzleinsdorfer Schlosspark
A landscape park north of Türkenschanzpark with old-growth oaks, a baroque schloss, fenced deer enclosure, hilltop meadows looking toward the Wienerwald. Vienna's classic "neighborhood-but-feels-rural" park — locals walk dogs here, tourists never find it. The south-facing hilltop meadow above the schloss (10 minutes from the gate) is one of the cleanest near-city panoramas, looking back at the dense bowl of inner Vienna with the Wienerwald wrapping behind your shoulders. A wooden bench and a single oak mark the spot; locals call it simply *die Wiese*.
*Sources: parks_squares, viewpoints*

### Schnitzlervilla (Sternwartestraße 71)
Schnitzler bought this villa in 1909 and wrote his great late novellas here — *Fräulein Else*, *Traumnovelle* (the basis for Kubrick's *Eyes Wide Shut*). The 1962 centenary plaque names him simply "der Dichter Arthur Schnitzler." The "Cottage" neighbourhood was the Austrian intellectual bourgeoisie's preferred retreat — Mahler, Freud, and Schnitzler within a 15-minute walk.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

## District 1190 — Döbling

### Karl-Marx-Hof (Heiligenstädter Straße 82–92)
1.1 km long — among the longest single residential buildings in the world (Karl Ehn, 1927–30). Six arched ochre gateways cut through the salmon-red façade; four 5-metre tower-like statues by Josef Riedl stand over the central tower. The silhouette deliberately echoes a Florentine palazzo to make workers' housing look like aristocracy. 1,382 apartments financed by a luxury tax on the rich; kindergartens, library, pharmacy, laundries all built in. Tram D terminates at its doorstep.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Nussdorfer Wehr- und Schleusenanlage (Heiligenstädter Brücke)
Otto Wagner's hydraulic-engineering Secessionist masterpiece (1894–99), where the Donaukanal splits off from the Danube. Two massive bronze-and-stone lions guard the lock on top of pillars; Wagner called the ensemble a "modern city gate." A three-storey Secession administrative building sits among the gears. Industrial architecture pretending to be a triumphal arch.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Mayer am Pfarrplatz — Beethoven's Heiligenstadt house (Pfarrplatz 2)
A wine tavern with a courtyard, blue Saint Florian statue at the corner, vines climbing the staircase. Beethoven lived here in 1817 trying to cure his deafness at the nearby spa. The Pfarrplatz is tiny — three buildings, a church, a fountain. It feels like a village.
*Sources: oddities*

### Eroica-Haus (Döblinger Hauptstraße 92)
Beethoven spent the summer of 1803 in Oberdöbling and on this stretch of Döblinger Hauptstraße he drafted the *Eroica* — the symphony that broke open the 19th century. (Historians debate the exact house, but this lane is where Symphony No. 3 was thought into existence.) Tram 37 stops one block away.
*Sources: cultural_literary*

### Setagayapark (Hohe Warte / Gallmeyergasse)
A pure Japanese garden designed in 1992 by Ken Nakajima, modeled on Kyoto's Shugakuin Palace garden. Bamboo Tokusabari gate, Yukimi-style stone lantern, tea house, koi pond, cascade. Twinned with Tokyo's Setagaya ward. In May, the maples and azaleas peak.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Wertheimsteinpark (Döblinger Hauptstraße 96)
Once a private garden of textile baron Friedrich von Arthaber (1835), donated to the city in 1908 with the legal requirement it remain a public park forever. A small pond is the remnant of the Krottenbach stream now culverted underground. The "Blind Garden" (1959), designed for scent and texture, is one of Europe's earliest sensory gardens.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Kahlenberg terrace & Stefaniewarte (Am Kahlenberg 2–4)
The 484 m hill from which Sobieski's Polish-Imperial cavalry rolled down on 12 September 1683 and broke the Ottoman siege — the cannon ridge is now a paved panoramic terrace looking straight down the Danube into the city. The 22 m Stefaniewarte (1887, named after Crown Princess Stephanie) is climbable for free outside hours when the gate is open; even from its base the parapet reads as the edge of a stage set. Bus 38A from U4 Heiligenstadt.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Sobieski-Kapelle exterior (Kahlenberg summit, beside St. Josef-Kirche)
A small chapel rebuilt 1906 on the spot where King Jan III Sobieski reportedly attended Mass before the charge — Polish national pilgrimage site, with a 1983 mosaic over the door commemorating the siege's 300th anniversary. The chapel is locked outside services; the exterior wall plaques in Polish and German, and the views west into the Wienerwald, are open air.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Kahlenberg ORF radio mast clearing (Höhenstraße)
A 165 m guyed steel transmission mast (1955), one of Vienna's tallest structures, rising from a forest clearing just behind the terrace. The mast is fenced but the clearing around it is a quiet GPS-friendly opening above the canopy — the red-and-white spire is visible from half of Vienna as a navigation beacon. Locals call it simply "der Sender."
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Leopoldsberg terrace & Leopoldskirche forecourt (Leopoldsberg 1)
The other northern hill, 425 m, with even cleaner sightlines: the Leopoldskirche (1693, rebuilt 1718) sits on the ruined foundations of the Babenberg castle where Leopold III ruled Austria from 1095 onward, before Vienna existed as a capital. The free forecourt terrace reads the Danube bend like a topographic model — you can see Bratislava on clear days. Bus 38A, then a 10-minute downhill walk from Kahlenberg via the Nasenweg ridge.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Nasenweg ridge path (Kahlenberg → Nussdorf)
A serpentine forest stair-trail dropping 350 vertical metres from Kahlenberg down to the Heiligenstadt vineyards in ~45 minutes. Named for its switchback "noses" cut into the spine of the hill. Repeated viewpoint clearings face south over the city; the descent ends at the Beethoven Heiligenstadt district. Beethoven walked variants of this trail during his deaf-cure summers.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Cobenzl panoramic terrace & vineyard belvedere (Am Cobenzl 94/96a)
The middle hill between Kahlenberg and the city, with a broad free terrace beside the former Schloss Cobenzl (now an event venue) — the view is lower and closer than Kahlenberg, so the city reads as a model rather than a map. Vineyards drop away in stripes; the Stephansdom spire lines up dead-centre. Vienna is the only world capital that runs its own municipal wine estate; the Weingut Cobenzl pavilion at the belvedere serves the wine pressed 50 metres downhill, with Bratislava visible 50 km east on clear days. Bus 38A stops at the door.
*Sources: viewpoints, niche*

### Am Himmel — Lebensbaumkreis (Himmelstraße 125)
A hilltop meadow above Grinzing whose name translates literally as "in heaven." Since 2004 it has held the *Lebensbaumkreis*, a circle of 40 native trees arranged on a Celtic calendar — each tree assigned to a birth-window, with a stone disc at the centre. Free, open day and night, with a 360° clearing view over the Wienerwald foothills.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Reisenbergwiese & Bellevuewiese (Am Cobenzl)
Two adjacent meadows north of Cobenzl whose paragliders launch from on still afternoons. The Bellevuewiese carries a small stone marker placed in 2006 — the spot where Freud, walking with Marie Bonaparte in summer 1900, supposedly joked he expected a plaque reading *"Here, on July 24, 1895, the secret of dreams revealed itself to Dr. Sigm. Freud."* It got the plaque a century later.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Beethoven-Ruhe / Beethovengang viewpoint (Heiligenstadt)
A wooded brook-side path Beethoven walked while drafting the Pastoral Symphony — the small clearing with an 1863 stone bust by Anton Paul Wagner ("Beethoven-Ruhe") opens onto a vineyard slope view back over Heiligenstadt. The path is still hedged with the same hornbeam-and-elder species, runs past stone benches; signposted from Pfarrplatz, 15 minutes uphill.
*Sources: viewpoints, niche*

### Grinzinger Hauptstraße / village square (Grinzing)
The Heurigen capital of Vienna — a cobbled village street of Buschenschank wine taverns marked by hung pine-branch wreaths (the *Buschen*) that signal "we have new wine this week," a code Joseph II legalised in 1784. Beethoven, Schubert, and Mark Twain all drank here; the village was annexed by Vienna in 1892 but still looks like a place that hasn't decided yet. Houses lean, vine-canopies arch the lanes, the 18th-century Pfarrkirche tower is the orientation point.
*Sources: niche*

### Sieveringer Straße Heurigen row (Sievering)
A quieter, more local alternative to Grinzing along Sieveringer Straße and Agnesgasse — vines climb walls between Biedermeier cottages and Heurigen courtyards that smell of *Sturm* in autumn. Sievering's vineyards crawl right up to the back gardens; the slope of the Latisberg starts at the end of the village.
*Sources: niche*

### Kahlenberg vineyard terraces (Höhenstraße)
The slopes between the Kahlenberg summit and Nussdorf are a working *Wiener Gemischter Satz* vineyard zone — the only DAC wine appellation defined by a city rather than a region. From the Höhenstraße you walk down through Riesling and Grüner Veltliner rows with the entire city laid out below; signposts identify grape varieties at each terrace. UNESCO-protected agricultural cityscape.
*Sources: niche*

### Krapfenwaldlbad terrace (Krapfenwaldgasse 65–73)
A 1923 hillside open-air pool above Grinzing with the city panorama as a backdrop — terraces stepped into the Vienna Woods slope. The pool charges admission, but the unfenced terrace road that loops around the upper edge is the highest free-access southern lookout on the Cobenzl ridge, with deck-chair-grade views of the city through a thin screen of beeches. The asphalt loop is open all year.
*Sources: viewpoints, niche*

### Hermannskogel / Habsburgwarte (542 m, Wienerwald rim)
The highest point of Vienna proper. A 27 m stone observation tower (1888, by Franz Neumann) on the Hermannskogel summit — confusingly *also* called Habsburgwarte like the western one. Open weekends April–October, free climb. Reference point for the original geodetic survey of Austria-Hungary — every imperial map's coordinates were anchored here. Reachable only by hiking trail (~1 hour from Cobenzl bus stop).
*Sources: viewpoints*

## District 1200 — Brigittenau

### Friedrich-Engels-Platz Hof (Engelsplatz-Hof)
One of Vienna's largest social housing complexes (Rudolf Perco, 1930–33) with stripped-classical towers at the corners — built by socialists in a city about to be authoritarian. Architecturally on the edge between Red Vienna and what came after. Layout symmetrical, axes long, archways huge. Often described as the architectural inverse of Karl-Marx-Hof: brutal where Ehn was warm. The gateway courtyards hold a series of 1932 social-realist reliefs by Rudolf Schmidt (workers, mothers, harvest scenes) that survived 1934's civil war and Austrofascist whitewashing; at the centre sits Anton Hanak's 1933 abstract bronze fountain "Aufbau" — three nude male figures bearing the basin on their shoulders.
*Sources: architecture, niche, outer_districts*

### Wallensteinplatz market
A long rectangular square with a Saturday farmers' market — named for General Wallenstein of the Thirty Years' War, who had a country house here when Brigittenau was floodplain. The 1898 Brigittakapelle sits at the south end; the 1898 cast-iron Jugendstil drinking fountain at the north end still works. A 1.5 km market promenade ends at U6 Jägerstraße.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Brigittakapelle (Forsthausgasse)
The chapel for which Brigittenau is named — a 1645 octagonal Baroque pilgrimage chapel to Saint Brigid of Sweden, the only octagonal Baroque chapel in Vienna. Sits in a small park; in summer hosts open-air concerts. Once stood completely alone on Danube floodplain; now ringed by 1900s tenements.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Nordbahn ghost line (Innstraße / Nordbahnstraße)
The original Vienna-Brno railway (1837, Austria's first steam line) terminated here; the station was bombed in 1945 and never rebuilt. The platform foundations, a section of original wrought-iron canopy, and one signal mast survive as a 19th-century railway ruin in a 21st-century construction site — currently being rewilded as a public park.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Hannovermarkt (Hannoverplatz)
A 1898 Gründerzeit market square with an iron-and-glass market hall, less famous than Karmelitermarkt but with a stronger Romanian/Bulgarian/Polish flavour from 2000s migration. The Friday spice stalls are the social hub of the immigrant inner-Brigittenau.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Umspannwerk Wien-Nord (Engerthstraße / Brigittenauer Lände)
A monumental 1929 substation by Eugen Kastner clad in fluted brick pilasters with bas-relief friezes representing electricity allegorically — a Genius with a thunderbolt over the entry. Listed as a heritage monument; still in service. One of the great unloved buildings of inter-war Vienna.
*Sources: infrastructure*

## District 1210 — Floridsdorf

### Karl-Seitz-Hof (Jedleseer Straße 66–94)
A semicircular Hof for 5,000 residents (~1,173 apartments) by Hubert Gessner, 1926–31 — same architect as Reumannhof and equally consciously palatial. Gessner gave it a baroque-style central courtyard with a curved colonnade, as if Schönbrunn had been rebuilt as workers' housing.
*Sources: architecture*

### Reichsbrücke (U1 over the Danube)
The current cable-stayed bridge (1976–80) replaces the original 1872–1876 structure that collapsed without warning into the Danube at 04:59 on 1 August 1976. A single bus was on it; the driver survived and the bus was salvaged and stayed in service until 1989. The longest stretch of the metro that is above water in Vienna. The cable-stayed deck carries the U1 metro upstairs and pedestrians on a side cantilever — the centerpoint of the cantilever is the geographic mid-Danube spot in Vienna, 25 m above the water. Look upstream and Bisamberg fills the horizon; look downstream and the Hungarian plain begins.
*Sources: architecture, infrastructure, viewpoints*

### Schlingerhof (Brünner Straße 34–38)
A Red Vienna housing complex (Hans Glaser, 1925–26) with one of the most theatrical entry archways in Vienna — a 15-metre brick gateway under a stepped tower-gable, framing an interior courtyard with sgraffito panels of workers. The Schlingerhof's covered market still runs Tuesday–Saturday; named for socialist politician Hans Schlinger.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Floridsdorfer Spitz (Am Spitz)
The triangular square where Floridsdorf's three main streets converge — anchored by the 1898 Floridsdorfer Pfarrkirche and the 1888 cast-iron market pavilion. A surprising piece of Belle Époque urban planning across the Danube; pure Habsburg-suburban texture.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Bruckhaufen Spinnerin am Kreuz (Mengergasse)
A small 18th-century wayside shrine called the "Spinning Madonna" — a stone column with a relief of Mary spinning, marking the path workers walked between Vienna and the Marchfeld farms. Folk legend says women who touched the column got better at spinning. Tiny, unguarded, easy to miss.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wasserpark (Floridsdorfer Wasserpark)
Vienna's only public park built around an active drinking-water reservoir — the 1910 brick Wasserspeicher 2 sits at the center, a Romanesque-revival fortress of pumps, ringed by a swan-and-lily lake. Cycling paths, a Hanak bronze ("Wasserträgerin," 1929), and surprisingly few tourists.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Bruckhaufen island & Donaumeisterhaus
The small Bruckhaufen quarter — a historic Danube boatmen's settlement that became an island when the Danube was straightened in 1875. Single-story 19th-century fishermen's cottages, one street, ends at the river. The 1830 Donaumeisterhaus (boat-master's house) still stands.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Donauinsel northern tip — Toter Grund
The northernmost km of Donauinsel narrows to a wild gravel point where the island's flood-channel rejoins the main Danube. Reed beds, a small wooden viewing platform built in 2018, and a clean horizon view of Bisamberg's vineyard stripes across the water. Almost no tourists make it this far north.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Donauinsel weirs — Einlaufbauwerk Langenzersdorf (northern tip)
Where the Neue Donau begins: a massive concrete inlet structure with seven steel sluice gates that open only during flooding to divert overflow from the main Danube into the relief channel. In normal weather the gates are sealed and the Neue Donau is a still lake. Walk to the island's north end and stand on top of a flood-control machine that protects Vienna from 100-year inundations.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Floridsdorfer Brücke (U6 over the Danube)
A 405-metre steel girder bridge (current structure 1978) carrying both road traffic and the U6 line — Vienna's only U-Bahn crossing of the main Danube where the train runs in the open on a road bridge. From the U6 window you get the full Danube panorama; from the Donauinsel below, you see the brutalist structural pylons that hold one of Europe's busiest tram-trains aloft.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Stammersdorfer Kellergasse (Stammersdorf)
A 1.5-km lane on the Bisamberg's southern slope lined with low-vaulted *Pressehäuser* — wine-press cottages built directly over hillside cellars dug into the loess. Vienna's largest contiguous wine-village zone, on the *left* bank of the Danube, which most tourists never reach. The Kellergasse architecture — single-storey, whitewashed, doorways for barrels rather than people — is preserved more strictly than in Grinzing.
*Sources: niche*

### Bisamberg — Elisabethhöhe (Stammersdorf)
Vienna's only proper northern wine hill that the city actually owns above 350 m. The Elisabethhöhe lookout on its eastern shoulder (358 m) is a small clearing with a wooden pavilion, reachable by tram 31 to Stammersdorf and a 45-minute climb through Heuriger vineyards. The view is unusual: most Vienna viewpoints look south at the city, this one looks across the Danube at the city *and* at Kahlenberg as a silhouette.
*Sources: viewpoints*

## District 1220 — Donaustadt

### DC Tower 1 (Donau-City-Straße 7)
Austria's tallest building, Dominique Perrault, 2013 — 250 m, 60 floors. The west façade is dead-flat mirror glass; the east façade is folded like crumpled foil. The fold means the building reads as a different shape from every direction — wide from the south, knife-edge from the east.
*Sources: architecture*

### Donauturm (Donauturmstraße 4)
Built for the 1964 Vienna International Garden Show — 252 m. Two revolving restaurants at 161 m and 169 m; each does a full rotation in 24 minutes — about one cappuccino. Lifts reach the platform in 35 seconds. On a clear day you see the Hungarian border.
*Sources: architecture, oddities*

### Donaupark — Iris Lake (Wagramer Straße)
A 25,000 m² artificial lake at the centre of a 63-hectare park, ecologically retro-fitted in 1993 to function as a natural water-treatment biotope: the marshy banks filter the water. Above the park stands the 252-m Donauturm. The open-air stage hosts free concerts May–September.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Donauinsel (21-km island)
A man-made island engineered 1972–88 as flood control — accidentally becoming Europe's longest urban beach. Reachable from U1 Donauinsel. Grill zones, water playgrounds, FKK areas (clothing-optional, by law), the legendary Sunken City bar district at the south end. Pure horizontal recreation.
*Sources: parks_squares*

### Goethehof (Schüttauplatz 5–11)
A 727-apartment Red Vienna complex (1928–30) on the Donaustadt side — quieter than Karl-Marx-Hof but architecturally important because it was one of the first Hofs built on the unfashionable east bank of the Danube, projecting the social-housing program across the river into territory the bourgeoisie considered swamp. Long curved façades with mosaic friezes.
*Sources: architecture*

### Donaupark Iris-See ridge
A low artificial ridge (~12 m) loops the north side of the Iris Lake — built from 1964 garden-show excavation spoil. The crest is the only spot where Donauturm, DC Tower 1, the UN Vienna campus, and the Karl-Marx-Hof silhouette across the Danube all appear in one frame. Free, signposted from U1 Kaisermühlen.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Donauinsel Copa Beach city panorama
The southern Copa Beach esplanade has a low concrete sea-wall facing back toward the inner city: from here, Donauturm, DC Tower 1, the cathedral spire, and the Wienerberg high-rises all line up across the water in late-afternoon light.
*Sources: viewpoints*

### Manfred Wakolbinger sculptures (Donauinsel)
Wakolbinger's stainless-steel tubular forms — bent like seaweed or planetary antennae — punctuate stretches of the Donauinsel sculpture path. They reflect the river and sky, looking different in every season; located between Reichsbrücke and Steinspornbrücke.
*Sources: niche*

### Donauinsel pedestrian bridges (Steinspornbrücke, Ponte Cagrana)
A chain of cyclist/pedestrian bridges added piecemeal 1985–2001 to stitch the 21-km artificial island to both banks. The Steinspornbrücke (a wooden-decked beam bridge near the southern tip) is the loneliest; Ponte Cagrana floats over the New Danube on cable-stayed pylons. The whole island is an engineered hydraulic safety device disguised as a beach, accessed by bridges that exist because the island itself exists.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Stadlauer Ostbahnbrücke (near Praterspitz)
A 1872 steel-truss railway bridge (rebuilt after WWII destruction, 1949) carrying the Ostbahn line east toward Bratislava — Austria's longest 19th-century iron truss. Approachable from the Donauinsel southern tip — you walk underneath the rust-coloured girders while freight trains rumble overhead; original 1870 plaques in German and Hungarian mark when the Imperial-Royal Eastern Railway crossed into Habsburg Hungary.
*Sources: infrastructure, outer_districts*

### Lobau wilderness gate (Saltenstraße / Esslinger Furt)
The western edge of the Lobau — a 23-km² wilderness floodplain forest, part of Donau-Auen National Park, within the 22nd district. Free entry through a small gate; reed beds, beavers, herons, the silted-up branches of the old Danube. The most genuinely wild place inside any major European city.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kaisermühlen old village (Schüttauplatz)
Before the UN City and the DC Tower, Kaisermühlen was a Danube fishermen's hamlet — and the village core still survives behind the towers: low cottages, a 19th-century parish church, the "Kaisermühlen-Blues" of Kottan-Detective fame. Walk in from Donauinsel U1 and the towers vanish in two minutes.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### UN City / VIC (Wagramer Straße 5)
Johann Staber's 1979 complex of six interconnected Y-shaped towers — UN's third headquarters, after New York and Geneva, on technically extraterritorial land. The plaza in front (Memorial Plaza) is publicly accessible: a bronze globe sculpture by Henry Moore-school sculptors and the 1979 Peace Bell donated by Japan, rung once a year on UN Day.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Aspern Seestadt
Vienna's largest new-town development (begun 2010, completion 2028+) — an entire district of 240 hectares built around a 5-hectare artificial lake, with the U2 line extended to serve it. The plan was developed by Madrid's Tovatt Architects + the city; streets are all named after women (Hannah-Arendt-Platz, Maria-Tusch-Straße). A 21st-century Vienna under construction.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Mühlwasser Auenpark
A linear park along an old Danube side-arm that survived the 1875 river-straightening — willow-shaded, with a 1900s wooden footbridge and a small wetland viewing platform. Beavers reintroduced 2010s, frequently visible at dusk. A pocket of pre-19th-century riverscape three U-Bahn stops from the DC Tower.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kaiserwasser locks (Alte Donau)
The chain of weirs and small locks that 1870s engineers used to convert a meandering Danube arm into the still, swimmable Alte Donau lakes. Walk the Kaiserwasser embankment from U1 Alte Donau and you see the cylindrical sluice gates that froze a river in 1875. The Alte Donau is officially "standing water" by hydraulic decree — a piece of nature legislated into existence by these locks.
*Sources: infrastructure*

### Strandbad Gänsehäufel (Moissigasse 21)
A 1900s island lido on a side-arm of the Old Danube — wooden cabanas, sandy beaches, 1920s functionalist pavilions, FKK section. Free outer perimeter walks around the bath show the period architecture. Vienna's working-class summer paradise, redesigned by Erich Franz Leischner in the 1950s after war damage.
*Sources: niche*

### Albert-Schultz-Halle / Eisarena Kagran (Attemsgasse 1)
A 1990s hexagonal-truss ice arena home to EHC Wien — visible from the U1 Kagran exit. Less architecturally famous than Stadthalle but the perimeter has Donaustadt's most concentrated 1990s-arena typology.
*Sources: niche*

## District 1230 — Liesing

### Wotruba-Kirche (Ottillingerplatz 1 / Rysergasse / Georgenberg)
Fritz Wotruba's brutalist church (1974–76) is 152 staggered, asymmetric concrete cubes piled on a 380 m hilltop — looks more megalithic monument than church. Wotruba was a leading Austrian sculptor; his church has no nave, no axis, no symmetry — just light entering through the chance gaps. The exterior plaza wraps the building with views east over the Liesing valley and west into the Wienerwald. Bus 60A from Hietzing, 15-minute uphill walk.
*Sources: viewpoints, outer_districts*

### Maurer Wald (Mauer forest)
A 6-km² beech-oak forest on Vienna's south-western edge, with the Anninger and Föhrenberge hills rising at its far end. Public hiking paths and signed Wanderwege. The Reisenberg clearing at the top has an old surveyor's triangulation stone (1893) and a long view back over the Wotruba church. You can hike from the brutalist church into proper woods in five minutes.
*Sources: viewpoints, outer_districts*

### Kalksburg Schloss & arboretum (Promenadegasse 3)
The 1745 Schloss Kalksburg — a small Baroque country palace once owned by the Liechtenstein family, now a Jesuit boarding school, with publicly accessible gardens and an arboretum of exotic trees (Lebanese cedar, ginkgo, sequoia). The chapel courtyard has a 1730 Baroque marble fountain.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Atzgersdorf old village core (Breitenfurter Straße)
The historic core of Atzgersdorf — Liesing absorbed it in 1938 — with a small village square, the 1740 St. Catherine parish church, and the "Atzgersdorfer Mühle" (the old miller's house with its working waterwheel preserved). Tram 60 stops at the square. Feels like a Lower Austrian village inside Vienna.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Liesinger Brauerei tower (Brauergasse)
The surviving 1840 brick tower of the former Liesing Brewery — closed in 1979 but the malthouse tower was preserved as an industrial landmark, with its original wrought-iron weathervane shaped like a hop flower. Now stranded inside a residential development; you can walk right up to the base.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Perchtoldsdorfer Heide
A protected dry-grassland steppe on the boundary with Perchtoldsdorf — orchids, bee-eaters, sand lizards. Like the Simmeringer Heide but warmer and richer; in May covered in yellow flax and pasque-flower. Vienna's south-western nature reserve, ten minutes by tram from a U6 station.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Kaiserin-Elisabeth-Allee (Mauer)
A 1.5-km tree-lined allée Empress Elisabeth used as her private riding path between Hermesvilla and the Mauer hills — 250 plane trees planted 1885, still standing. Now a quiet residential street, but the proportions are imperial: 14 metres wide, dead straight, with old stone milestones every 200 metres.
*Sources: outer_districts*

### Wasserbehälter Rosenhügel (Atzgersdorfer Straße)
The 1873 covered reservoir terminus of the First Mountain Spring Pipeline — a low brick structure topped by a public park (the Rosenhügel itself). You walk over 145,000 m³ of Alpine water without knowing it. The original neoclassical pump house is still there at the edge, in the strict architectural register of 1870s railway stations.
*Sources: infrastructure*
